# THE TEN THOUSAND: STORY TWO, THE GOLD

---

> *From The Traveler's Primer, printed at Sill, one crown.*
>
> One stroke of the gate bell signifies a living return. Two signifies a body. The bell is rung at once and without ceremony, and travelers are asked to understand that this is a kindness. Families waiting at Gate One have several times petitioned for it to be rung more slowly. The petitions were refused and the reasons given have not been written down.

---

# CHAPTER ONE

They rang the bell twice at Gate One on a Tuesday.

Twice is a body. Once is a living return. The bell's on a post by the arrivals board and the clerk rings it the moment the shape on the plinth stops moving, because there are families on those benches in all weathers and the kindest thing anyone ever worked out to do for them was to tell them fast.

Gate One is a railway station built round a door. Slate board, tiered benches, a roof over most of it and a stove that goes out. People come and sit there with no information at all, because a gate is not a timetable, and the ones who come every week are known to the clerks by name and are not discussed.

---

Nell Coombe was on the third bench. Ninth time in a month.

It wasn't him. She told me all this afterwards, in the order she'd lived it, which is not the order I'm telling it in. The wrapping was wrong for a start, black and waxed and stitched at the head, and there was a woman four seats along who stood up when she saw it and made a noise, so at least it was somebody's.

---

Nell got the tram back into the city and asked at the Lazaret and got sent nine miles the wrong way, and found me on the steps of the receiving hall at about eleven at night with the wind coming up off the flats.

She'd been standing there long enough that the shoulders of her coat had gone stiff with salt. That tells you she hadn't wanted to go inside and ask again.

"What should I call you?"

"Sexton's fine."

"My brother's stuck."

Most people take four minutes to get to that.

---

We went into the tea shop by the tram stop that stays open for the night traffic, and she put her hands round the cup and didn't drink any of it.

"He went out on the fourth of last month. Gate 1,714. He's nineteen."

"1,714 hasn't been walked."

"No."

"Nobody's ever been through it."

"Two people have," she said. "Gil and Robin Tarr."

---

I got it out of her in order, because in order is the only way anything makes sense.

Gil turned eighteen and did what every optimistic boy in this city does, which is walk into Verrick's with his father's name and sign the paper. Verrick's is above board. They're old, they're well capitalized, and they've never cheated anybody in a way the courts could get hold of. There's a painting in their front hall of a Verrick's crew standing on the Salt in clean coats. I have been on the Salt for nineteen years and I have never once seen a crew in a clean coat, and I have gone as far as finding out who to write to about it.

The deal is this. They buy your stone, which you could not afford in nine years of any honest work. They put you on a seasoned crew that knows what it's doing, so you don't die in your first summer, which most of the ones who go out alone do. They feed you, they insure you, they teach you the trade properly.

And they take ninety of every hundred crowns you bring back, for as long as you're theirs.

That isn't robbery. That's the price of a stone plus the price of not being dead, and I've watched a lot of men in bars work out that it's fair and hate it anyway.

Gil did fourteen months and thirty-one crossings and got good. Then he did the arithmetic everybody does at about month twelve, which was that he'd earned Verrick's four thousand crowns and had eleven in a tin under his bed.

So he left, with Robin Tarr, and a third boy who was meant to be there and wasn't.

"They had a name," Nell said. "The Dragonflies. They had badges made, three of them, tin, off a stall in the Ninth. Toby's mother said no. He's at the customs house now. He still wears his."

---

Two boys and one stone.

You can tether a man home on somebody else's stone. People do it. It works. The reason nobody in this trade will look you in the eye while they explain it is that the man on the other end hasn't got a clock, hasn't got a say, and hasn't got a second chance if the man holding it does something stupid.

They couldn't afford two. So they took one and told each other it was the same thing, and it very nearly is.

"Whose stone?"

She looked at the table.

"My father's."

---

I'll set this out plainly, because it's the whole story and everything afterwards is people behaving reasonably about it.

Ellis Coombe went out through a gate in the four hundreds eleven years ago and died there, and eleven months later he came home through the same door wrapped in cloth that wasn't Sill cloth, with a stone bound against his sternum under a folded pad.

The gate was honest. Somebody out there gave up the only thing they had.

That stone paid for a funeral and a headstone and it could have paid for a great deal more, and their mother didn't sell it. She wore it on a cord for eleven years and never spent a crossing on it. When her son turned nineteen and told her he was going into business for himself, she took it off and put it in his hand.

"She said it would keep him honest," Nell said. "That was the word. Honest. I don't know if she meant it the way it sounded."

"How long have they been out?"

"Thirty-one days."

---

"What have you been told?"

"That windows are hours. Sometimes days. The clerk said he'd heard of one that ran eight days and that was a story people told." She turned the cup round. "So they're dead."

"No. It means nobody knows, which is a different thing, and I'd like you to hold on to the difference because it's going to be all you've got for a while."

She waited.

"Nobody's been through that door," I said. "So nobody knows what it does. Every number you've been quoted comes off the two thousand doors we've walked, and there's no reason on this earth the two thousand and one hundred and fourteenth should behave like any of them. It might run a month. It might run a year. That's not me being kind, that's the state of the knowledge, and it's why the Order pays what it pays for the answer."

"And if it's already gone clear and they weren't touching it?"

"Then they're standing on a world with a rock and no way back, and they're alive, and they'll stay alive as long as whatever's out there lets them, and I'll go and get them."

She started crying then, which people do at the strangest moments, and it's almost never at the bad news. It's at the first sentence that has a plan in it.

---

"I've got fifty-eight crowns," she said, when she'd finished. "And a chain of my mother's. I know it isn't near it. I asked what a crossing costs before I came."

"Keep the chain. Give me six now and I'll write you a receipt."

"Six."

"Six is a real price and it's the price. And you're going to hear the rest of this before you decide whether you're grateful, because I'd rather you didn't have to work it out later on your own."

I got out my notebook, which I didn't need, because I've known the shape of that list since I was younger than her brother. The notebook is for the other person. A number that comes out of a book looks like it came from somewhere, and a number that comes out of a man's mouth looks like he made it up, and both of those are the same number.

"The Order pays for the first filed account of a gate nobody's walked. Air, water, ground, people, the state of them, and the window. When 1,714 went on the list it was twelve hundred crowns."

"That's an enormous amount of money."

"It's nineteen hundred now."

She looked up.

"It goes up," I said. "That's the system and it's not a secret and it's printed in a book at the gate for anybody to read. A gate nobody's walked pays a figure. Every year nobody walks it, the figure goes up. And every time a crew goes in and doesn't come out, it goes up faster, because now the Order knows something is out there and knows it's worth more than it was."

She sat very still.

"Your brother went in on the fourth of last month and didn't come out," I said. "The clerk crossed out the old number in the fourth month after and wrote a new one. That's the job, he's meant to do it, and he'd have hated doing it."

"So Gil made it worth your while."

"Gil made it worth seven hundred crowns more than it was worth in the summer. And I'd like you to understand that this is exactly the machine that got him. The list is out there dangling money at anybody with nothing, and the only reason it's dangling that much is that other people with nothing went first. The Order will tell you it's the only honest way to price a thing nobody knows anything about." I put the notebook away. "They're right, and I've never once heard anyone say it out loud in front of a family."

She was quiet for a while. Then she said, "Would you go if it was still twelve hundred?"

"I'd have thought about it for longer."

"That's not an answer."

"No," I said. "It isn't."

---

# CHAPTER TWO

Corrigan's is three streets in from the Salt Gate and smells of tar and new hemp and the particular hot dust that comes off a line under load.

She had the book out before I'd got the door shut.

It's the ledger of every line she's ever sold, and there's a rule about it everybody in this city knows and nobody says out loud: when the buyer doesn't come back she rules a line under the name. The book is two thirds ruled.

She turned to the fourth of last month. *G. Coombe. R. Tarr. Ninety feet, four strand, rings and clips.*

No line under it.

"Thirty-one days," I said.

"I can count."

"Most people would have ruled it."

"I'd have to look at it after." She put her thumb on the entry and left it there. "Robin tested his own splices on my frame. Made me stand and watch, twice, because he wanted somebody to say they were wrong. I've had four hundred boys through that door and I can count on one hand the ones who wanted to be told they were wrong."

"What did they buy?"

"Everything you'd buy if you had one stone between two and were calling that a plan." She went back a page, then another, then further, which is what she does when she's not finished. "Fourth of last month. I sold a hundred and sixty feet that week. Look at that. Four of them since then and I've ruled two."

"Corrigan."

"They bought good line," she said. "That's what I'm telling you. They bought the best line in the shop and they paid for it and they didn't haggle, and I've had to look at that too."

---

I own two stones.

One is welded into my left forearm under a coin of burn scar. It's the size of a pocket watch, where every stone in existence is the size of a thumbnail, and it's cracked and can't be read and can't come out and can't be assayed. That's been the arrangement for twenty years.

The other is ordinary and clear and cost me four years of money that should have been rent, which is most of the answer to why a man who works constantly has nothing. It's got exactly one use, which is going on somebody else's body, because the gates won't let anybody carry two.

Two stones. So I could take one man.

I wanted two.

---

Here's the arithmetic, because it's the decision the whole trip turned on and I'd rather it wasn't mistaken for a preference.

Alone, anything that puts you down puts you down for good. There's no second version of you to go and find help, because there isn't any help, because you're the only person on the world.

Two is better and it's what most crews run, and what two gets you is that one of you can be hurt and the other can still work the problem. What it doesn't get you is anybody watching the ground while you work it.

Three is where a crew actually starts. Three means one holds and two do the job.

The trouble with a third is that a third needs a stone, and there's one place in the world you get one, and their terms are the account, and the account was the reason I was going.

And the other trouble is that I didn't know what I was buying. Nobody ever says that part. On a surveyed gate you read the file and you hire for the file: if it's cold you take somebody who's worked cold, if there's water you take somebody who swims. There was no file. I was picking kit and people for a place I couldn't describe in a single word.

So I took strength, and I took a climber, on the grounds that those are the two things you can't send for once you're out there, and I told myself that whichever one turned out to be useless would be carried by the other.

They were both useless on the first day and both of them saved it by the fourth. That's about as well as this ever goes.

---

The lending floor is a long cold room with a counter down the middle and no chairs, and the reason there are no chairs is that they don't want you comfortable while you decide.

Tollis Vane is the chief assayer. Small, tidy, seamless charcoal, a reading lens on a thin arm behind his ear, and he is the most feared unarmed man in Sill.

Here's why, because outsiders never get it.

A stone has a number of crossings left in it and nobody knows the number. It varies, it can't be predicted, and one day it stops partway through a return and freezes there, faintly lit, and that's an ember, and if it happens while you're out then nothing is coming for you. Your crew snaps away and you're standing where they were.

Wear can be read. Faintly. By people the Order trains and nobody else.

So Vane looks at a stone for as long as it takes to boil an egg and says a number, and that number sets your insurance, your credit, your crew rate and whether anybody will hire you. And the number is a guess. Everyone in that room knows it's a guess. It goes on the paper as a fact and the paper is what exists afterwards.

"Rental. Ordinary. One companion, unwalked gate."

"Which."

"1,714."

He wrote it down twice.

He rolled three out on the felt and looked at each of them.

"Forty."

"And the second?"

"Thirty. Perhaps thirty-five."

He took longer over the third.

"Sixty," he said. "Sixty is what I will write."

That's the whole man in five words and it's the reason the trade is frightened of him. He didn't say the stone had sixty crossings in it. He said sixty was the number that would exist.

I have watched a man's insurance, his crew rate and very nearly his engagement move four inches sideways because Tollis Vane had a settled morning. Nobody has ever complained. There is a form for complaining. I have looked at it, and the first thing it asks you for is the assayer's number, which he writes.

I took the sixty. Two hundred against the account, plus the standard terms, and the standard terms are the part that matters.

The Order lends you a stone and owns what you learn. All of it. You come back and you file, and if you hold something out and they find out, that's lying to the archive, and lying to the archive is the one crime in this city the Order punishes in ways the courts may not review.

I signed it knowing exactly what I was going to do at the end, and I'd like that on the record so nobody thinks it crept up on me.

---

Cassandra Lorde runs the best crew in Sill. The trade calls her the Tariff. I'm the only one who calls her Cas, which she permits the way you permit a dog on the furniture.

She isn't a rival. A rival wants what you've got. Cas is a counterparty, which means she'll help you or gut you at the price she quoted, and she has never once misquoted.

"I want Dray. Fourteen days, probably less."

"He isn't for hire by the day."

"He is at four times the rate. I've paid it before."

She got the ledger out and wrote nothing in it, which is how she thinks.

"1,714," she said. "Vane's floor talks, and your girl asked at eleven doors before she got to yours. Four times the day rate. And I read the account before it goes in."

"No."

"You've said no before I've finished, which means you've decided it's grubby." She put the pen down. "It isn't grubby. Listen to the actual thing I'm asking for. I don't want the stone, I don't want salvage, I don't want a share of your bounty, and I'm not asking you to change a word of it. I want to read the true thing before four hundred other people read it, because four hundred other people reading it is what makes it worthless to me. Once it's in the archive anyone can act on it. Before it's in the archive, one person can."

"And that person funds an expedition to a world I've just told you is habitable."

"Yes. Obviously. That's what I do, and you've known what I do for eleven years, and you keep making a face about it." She almost smiled. "You've been letting other people work out what your information's worth since you were twenty-five and calling that not being interested in money. It isn't. It's being bad at it."

I took the terms. She's never broken one, and there are nights that bothers me more than if she lied.

Four times the day rate for Dray is robbery. It is also exactly what Dray is worth. I have never once got those two facts to sit in the same sentence without one of them standing up and leaving.

---

Sparrow found me on the steps of the lending floor, which meant she'd been watching the lending floor, which meant she'd been watching it for four days.

She's sixteen, or says she is. Cut-down adult's coat with the cuffs turned twice. Lime hair with two inches of grown-out root, done herself, badly.

"You need a third and I want to say why it should be me and I've got four things."

"Sparrow."

"One. I'm light. Two. I climb, and I don't mean I've climbed, I mean I get sent up the frame at Corrigan's when the frame's loaded because I'm the only one who'll do it." She had it off by heart and you could hear the joins. "Three. I've been out four times and none of them went wrong, and I know that's four times and I know what four times is worth."

"What's four?"

She stopped.

"Four was going to be about how I don't eat much," she said. "I took it out last night. It sounded like begging."

That's the thing about her. She rehearses, and when you knock her off it she goes back to the top and starts again rather than answering, and that's exactly what sixteen sounds like. But she'd cut her own worst line and she'd known why.

"You haven't got a stone," I said.

"No."

"Say the rest of it."

"Most people haven't got a stone," she said. "I'm never going to have one. Nobody in my family's ever had one. The only way I ever go through a door is if somebody rents one and puts it in my name, and that's the whole reason I keep asking you, and I've asked you four times, and I'm going to keep asking."

"Fifth time," I said. "Get your kit."

She didn't whoop. She went white and nodded twice, fast, like a woman accepting a delivery, and then went off down the street at a dead run.

The stone in her name was the sixty. I've thought a great deal since about the fact that the first stone that girl ever wore was one I rented from a man who was guessing.

---

The train east takes most of a day.

The Order has a rate on that line. The Order has a rate on everything. I asked once, in writing, whether a man traveling on an Order contract travels at the Order's rate, and a clerk wrote back to explain that the rate belongs to the Order, and that I am a separate party to the Order, and that the Order cannot extend to a separate party a rate that is not the separate party's. Sod a clerk. I have paid full fare to that gate about four hundred times and I have kept every ticket.

The rail runs along the front of the field and stops a little past Gate 2,000, and everything this city has ever done lives inside those two thousand doors. Every survey, contract, crew and bounty. That's the known world and it's a fifth of the field at most. Past the rail you walk, and the line keeps going into the haze and doesn't thin and doesn't curve.

Out that far you can see the thing properly. White ground, white sky, no horizon worth the name, and arches standing in a line running east until they're too small to count. Every one identical. Same height, same width, same pale unweathered stone, same round disc set above the opening that nobody has ever explained. The openings hold a flat pale sheet like standing water seen edge-on. It doesn't ripple and it doesn't show you anything back.

And every single one has a hut beside it with a light in it and somebody in it with a book, in February and in August, all the way to wherever it ends.

Which means the Order has been to the end and knows the number.

They've never said it. Four men have asked in front of me in my life and they all got the same sentence, word for word, courteous and immediate, and it's the sameness that gets into you afterwards.

---

The clerk at 1,714 was a big soft-faced man called Hollis, six years at that door, two names in his book.

He talked without stopping for ten minutes and then apologized for it, and then did it again. Six years at a door nobody walks through and two names in the book, and the man had more to say than anybody I have ever met. You could put that together yourself.

"They asked the right questions, the two of them. I said so at the time. Good questions, and they wrote the answers down, which most don't." He got the kettle on. "Nothing's ever come out of that door. No body, no stone, no letter, nothing, not in three thousand years, which is what I told them. That's not the same as nothing being there. The tall one said that. Robin. That's near enough word for word what he said, and I've thought about it since."

Then he did the part of the job that pays.

He turned the book round on the table so we could see the bounty list, and there was 1,714 in the column with nothing beside it. No survey line, no population, no window.

Just a number, and a figure, and the figure had been crossed out three times.

Twelve hundred in a hand thirty years old. Fourteen hundred under it. Sixteen hundred in a third hand. And then nineteen, fresh, in Hollis's own careful clerk's writing, dated the fourth month after two boys went in and didn't come out.

He didn't say anything about it and neither did I.

"Two hundred separate for a measured window," he said. "That's on any unwalked door. That one's been claimed before by people who weren't there to claim it, if you follow, because a body comes back and you count the days, and that's a measurement, and somebody gets paid for it."

He took our names. He took the contract number and the gate authority. He asked who to notify. I said nobody. Dray said his sister on Blackrail. Sparrow gave an address in the Four Hundreds and then changed it to a different one.

Outside, the foot of 1,714 was bare salt.

At a working gate the arch is heaped with what crews left behind, and the clerk keeps the good pieces indoors and shows them to whoever's going through, and some of those huts have four generations of it in them. This one had nothing propped against the leg. Nothing burned into a board. No rotted frame, no sacks, no line.

Sparrow came and stood next to me and looked at it and didn't say anything, which I hadn't expected of her.

---

We went out to the plinth an hour before the light went.

Sparrow had hers in her fist with her thumb over it, tight, which is what everybody does at a plinth and what nobody has ever needed to be taught. It does nothing. You cross and it binds and that's that, and you could carry it in your hat.

I've got nothing to hold. Mine's under the skin and it's been there twenty years, and I put my palm flat over the scar anyway, every time, for the same reason she does it.

"Once more," I said.

"The color's the calendar," she said. "The knock's the alarm."

"Go on."

"Color tells you roughly. Days, hours, near the end. It doesn't tell you when." She had it word for word off some rigger's wall. "The stone tells you when. It goes warm, and then it thrums, and that's the knock, and you've got about two minutes. Get it on bare skin. Get hold of your people. Count."

"How long's yours?"

"I don't know. It's rented."

That's the part that got me, and I hadn't thought of it until she said it. Everybody in this city knows their own knock to four or five seconds the way you know your own handwriting. Hers was a stranger's.

"Then count a hundred and take it as ninety," I said. "And say the rest."

"Skin for people, attachment for things. A person comes home if they're on your bare skin. A thing comes home if it's held or hooked or tied to you and it doesn't care about cloth. Bag on my belt goes. Man on my rope doesn't."

"So what's the rope for?"

"Keeping him where I can reach him in two minutes."

"There's about nine a year who forget that," I said, "and they don't get to be wrong twice."

Mine knocks early. Mine knocks when it hasn't got anything to say, twice in an hour sometimes, and once in twenty years it didn't knock at all and simply took me out of a room in the middle of a sentence.

So I'm the only man in Sill who doesn't get the two minutes, and I've built a career on top of that, and I'd rather not be asked how.

Then you take hold of whatever's coming with you, and you check each other, and you walk into the sheet.

---

There's no curtain and no resistance. It's the exact feeling of stepping off a curb you didn't know was there. Your foot goes down further than you expected, one time, and by the time you've finished flinching you're somewhere else, and the light is wrong, and the air is a different temperature.

Except my foot didn't come down.

It went down and kept going.

---

We came out of a doorway in the face of a cliff and there was nothing under any of us, and we fell about five hundred feet.

It took nearly a minute.

I want to be careful here, because this is the single most important thing about that world and it ought to land the way it landed on us, which was slowly, and with a great deal of time to think.

We didn't drop. We descended, boots first, coats going up around us, at about the speed of a man walking downstairs, and the gold came up past us in layers, and my hat came off and stayed level with my shoulder the whole way down and I watched it go.

Dray made a sound I've never heard him make.

Sparrow started laughing at about two hundred feet and couldn't stop, and hers is a good laugh, and it went out into all that gold and didn't come back.

I had time to get both watches out and check them, and I did, because I'm the man I am.

Then the ground came up as gently as everything else and we landed in powder up to the ankle, in a warm gold fog, five hundred feet under the only door home.

Nobody said anything for a bit.

"Right," I said. "New rules."

---

# CHAPTER THREE

There's a thing you do on arrival and it isn't mystical and anybody can learn it. I do it in the same order every time and I've done it forty-odd times and I did it that day standing in ankle-deep powder with my hat coming down somewhere behind me.

Air first, because if the air's wrong nothing else matters and you've got about four breaths to find out. This air was warm and dry and tasted of chalk and it was fine. Then light, ground, water, growing things, and whether anything's looking at you.

The hat came down while I was on ground. It landed upright, nine feet off, and sat there like it had walked. I went and fetched it and put it on and nobody said a word, and I have decided since that they were being polite.

Then the two questions that pay.

What era. Not to be clever about it. You want to know what these people can make, because what they can make tells you what they'll want, what they'll trade, and what they'll do about a stranger. A place with mills and rail is a place that'll sell you a bed. A place with flint is a place where you're the most valuable object for a hundred miles and everybody's already worked that out.

And what's different. Every world runs on its own laws and most of the differences are small and one of them is always enormous, and the enormous one will kill you inside a day if you haven't named it.

I already had that one. It had taken a minute to explain itself and it had been very patient about it.

---

There's a fifth thing and I've stopped putting it in the list because it's never once been wrong and a thing that's never wrong isn't a check, it's just weather.

Everybody speaks Sill.

Not well. Nine generations on a world with no visitors and it comes back rotted, vowels flattened, half the tenses gone and full of words for things Sill has never seen. You have to listen hard for the first hour and then your ear finds it. But it's Sill, and it's Sill on worlds that have never heard of each other, and it has been like that for three thousand years.

Nobody knows why. There are four explanations and they're all religion. The Order's is that everybody came from somewhere the same a long way back, before the doors or because of them, and the Order's position on this is a guess in a good coat.

The Return's the same. There's a hand-sign for it that works nearly everywhere and nobody taught it to anybody.

So you walk through a door into a place no person from this city has ever stood, and a woman asks you what you want, and you understand her.

I stopped finding that comforting a long time ago.

---

Here's what we had.

The light was gold. Not gold-tinted, gold the way weather is gray: a warm heavy haze standing in the air with the sun somewhere behind it, and you could look straight at the sun without flinching. You could see maybe four hundred yards and then the world just softened and stopped.

The ground was powder, fine as flour, and it didn't settle. That's the sentence I'd give the Order if they only let me have one. Every step we took put up a slow bloom of dust that rose to about chest height and then hung there, and it was still hanging there an hour later when I looked back and could see exactly where we'd walked, a line of standing gold smoke going off toward the cliff.

There was water, in a shallow channel, moving slowly and wrong. It came over a lip and fell in ropes instead of sheets, and took its time about it.

And a mile up, there were things.

Enormous ones. Gauzy, pale, the size of a market hall, drifting along in the high gold with parts of them opening and closing very slowly. Four or five in sight at once. They were so far up and so unhurried that you kept forgetting to be frightened, and then remembering.

"Are those alive?" Sparrow said.

"Yes."

"How do you know?"

"They're not all going the same way."

---

The first rule was easy and we had it inside ten minutes, and it's the one I'd put in the file in the first line.

Nothing that leaves your hand comes back to it.

Dray dropped a spike, on purpose, to watch. It went down at about the speed of a leaf and landed nine feet from where he let go of it, because he'd let go with a little sideways in his fingers and nine feet is what a little sideways buys you here.

Sparrow spat, and then had to move.

The second rule cost us a lamp.

I got the storm lamp out because the haze was thick and I wanted to know how far it would throw, and struck it, and the flame stood up for about two seconds and then folded over on itself and went out in a small ball of its own smoke.

I did it four more times before I understood. Four. Nineteen years in the trade, standing on a world nobody has ever stood on, striking a lamp over and over at a problem that had already given me the answer the first time and been perfectly clear about it.

A flame needs the hot air to go up and the cold to come in underneath, and it needs it to happen briskly. Here it doesn't happen briskly. The smoke sits on the flame like a hand over a mouth and that's the end of it.

Which meant no lamp. On a world with a haze in it and no horizon.

"Put that in the file," Sparrow said.

"That's the file," I said. "That one's worth two hundred on its own and we've been here twelve minutes."

---

We found the first loop-post an hour later and that's when I knew there were people.

It was a stake of something pale and woody driven into the powder, about waist high, with a dozen cords whipped to it in a collar, each ending in a short hard toggle.

I've been unpacking the belongings of dead strangers for nineteen years and I can tell Sill work from the other kind in bad light without thinking, and this was better than Sill work. Not stronger. Better. Somebody who'd been doing knots for a thousand years and had never had anything else to be clever about.

I cut four feet of it off and brought it home for Corrigan. She has since taken it apart and put it back together in front of me twice, without being asked, and told me both times that it is showing off. She keeps it on the wall behind the frame.

There was no metal in it anywhere.

"That's a button," Sparrow said.

She was right and it took me a shamefully long moment. A toggle on a cord, pushed through a loop and turned crosswise. That's a button. That's the same object your coat has on it, doing the same job, holding a full-grown adult against a post.

Because a full-grown adult here weighs about as much as a hat.

---

The village was another two hours and we heard it before we saw it, which was the first genuinely strange thing, because what we heard was singing and it was coming from directly overhead.

Then the haze thinned and there were towers.

Nine of them, the tallest maybe two hundred feet, and they were the thinnest structures I have ever seen standing up. Lattices of pale stone, cut in blocks the size of a loaf, drilled and lashed, going up and up in a taper, and every one of them guyed out in eight directions on cords that ran down to stake collars a hundred yards out.

Nothing that thin has any business existing. It exists because nothing here weighs anything. A block that would break your foot in Sill can be moved with two fingers, and a wall that would fall over in Sill just doesn't.

And nets. Between the towers, on frames, at the tops, hanging off booms. Hundreds of yards of fine net, and people on the tower tops working them, and that was the singing.

They net the air. The haze is full of drifting stuff, seeds or spores or something between, and it comes across the plain on the slow air, and they hang out net and comb it and eat what's in it. There's your agriculture. No field, no plow, no season you could set a calendar by.

They can't dig. That's the other half of it and I want it in the file too. Put a hole in that powder and it fills itself, because the dust never settles and there's no weight in a spade and there's no water to hold a wall. So there's no mining, and no deep anything, and no cellar, and no grave.

They can't smelt, because they can't keep a fire. Which means no forge, no nails, no coin, and no tax collector, and I have thought about that last one more than is good for me.

Which means that everything on that world is rope, stone, bone, and wood, and I saw perhaps three pounds of worked metal in eleven days, all of it small, all of it worn the way this city wears a wedding ring.

---

Nobody walks in a straight line there and nobody walks unhooked.

You go up a tower in four bounds, from lashing to lashing, with a hand line running through a ring at your waist, and you come down in about eleven seconds and you land like a cat. Children do it before they can speak properly. Old women do it with the shopping.

Everything is furred with loops. The towers, the posts, the walls, the net booms, and every garment anybody wears. The front of a coat there is a mat of loops worn shiny with use, and you clip to a house the way you'd lean on a wall.

The standing rule is two points of contact before you let go of the first. They say it to their children in the singsong way we say look both ways, and I heard it about four hundred times in eleven days, and on the ninth day I watched a man sneeze on a boom eighty feet up with one point of contact and go off it sideways.

He came down slowly, and about two hundred feet out, in open powder, laughing, and walked back. Falling isn't the danger there.

Going up and not stopping is the danger there.

---

They came out to meet us and the first man to reach me took a short cord off his own coat and toggled it to a loop on my rope harness before he said a word.

He did it the way you'd shake a hand. Quick, businesslike, faintly embarrassed.

I have thought about that a great deal since. It means *I have hold of you*. It's the first thing you say to a stranger on a world where the only real death is drifting, and they've been saying it to each other for so long that nobody there could tell me why.

I gave it back, with a bit of my own line, and got it wrong, and a girl of about nine fixed it for me without being asked and with an expression of total contempt.

Then the man said something I couldn't follow, and pointed off into the gold, and said a word I could.

The word was *cutters*.

---

They were four hundred yards out, on the top of a half-built tower, and they were working.

That's what stopped me. I've done a lot of retrievals and I've got a set of pictures in my head for what the end of one looks like, and they're all of somebody sitting down.

Gil Coombe was nineteen and burned brown and thin as a rope, in the remains of a Verrick's-issue jacket with the sleeves gone, sixty feet up, cutting a fouled net line with a Corrigan hook knife while three of the locals held the boom.

He did it in one draw. There was a noise from below like a small cheer.

They'd been calling for them most days for a month. Steel is the whole story. There's nothing sharp on that world but flaked stone and worked bone, and the entire economy is cordage, and cordage goes wrong constantly, and a boy who can part a two-inch cable in one pull without sawing at it for a quarter of an hour is not a guest. He's a public utility.

They shout up the tower for them. The word means cutters and they use it as a name.

Robin Tarr was at the foot with a book.

---

He saw us first. He was sitting on a block with the book open on his knees and a stick of something charred in his fingers, and he looked up and his face did about four things.

Then he stood up and shouted, "GIL," in a voice that came apart in the middle.

And Gil came down that tower in eleven seconds without touching most of it, and hit the ground running, and stopped four feet short of me because he didn't know me.

"Nell sent me," I said.

He put both hands over his face.

Robin got to us a moment later and said, quite calmly, "You have to be careful in the dust, sir, if you'll forgive me, because if you go down on the flat you can put half a day's work into the air and nobody thanks you," and then he sat down in the powder and cried like a child.

---

I'd been on that world about seven hours at that point and I'd already worked out that they weren't in a cell and weren't being kept, and I want to be clear about how that changes a job.

When somebody's held, you're solving one problem. When they're not held, you find out you were never solving that problem at all.

So I asked the question I always ask second, because the first question is always are you hurt.

"Where's the stone?"

Gil didn't answer.

Robin pointed, straight up, at about thirty degrees off the vertical.

"There," he said.

---

It took me a while to see it and then I couldn't stop seeing it.

Thirty feet up. A little chip of clear nothing in all that gold, turning over very slowly, catching the light about once every four seconds and going out again. If the light hadn't caught it I'd have looked at that patch of sky all day and seen sky.

It had been up there thirty-one days.

"It went over in the first minute," Gil said. He said it in the flat voice people use for the sentence they've been saying to themselves in the dark for a month. "We came down off the cliff. It was in my hand. I put my hand out to steady Robin and it went off my palm and it went up, and I jumped after it, and jumping puts you eleven feet in the air here and I've had a month to learn that and I learned it right then."

"And it's been there since."

"It's never been more than about forty feet up," Robin said. "It's never come lower than twenty-six. The air here isn't wind, it's a sort of slow river, and it runs, and it turns, and it comes back."

He held up the book.

"It comes back," he said. "It comes back every eight days."

---

I want to say something about Robin Tarr here, because I got him wrong for the first hour and I'd like the record to show that I got him wrong.

He talked too fast and he smiled too much and he was all knees and elbows and enthusiasm with the stuffing coming out, and when he first opened that book on my knee I thought, this is a boy who has gone out of his mind in a gentle direction and made himself a calendar of it.

Then I read it.

Thirty-one days of observation. Time by count, since their watches had stopped being any use in the first week. Height by comparison against a tower he'd measured himself in cut blocks. Bearing off three fixed towers, taken twice a day. Wind by smoke, and by the way he'd made smoke was to hold a handful of powder up and let it go, and there's a column of numbers for how long it took to come down.

There's a page where he'd worked out that the air over the plain moves in a great slow ring, and the ring takes eight days, and he'd tested it eleven times.

There's another page where he'd been wrong about it and had gone back and marked all his earlier entries with a small honest *no*.

I've read Order surveys with less in them. I've written Order surveys with less in them.

"Nobody's ever going to see this," Robin said, watching me read it, "so I might as well say it. It's the best thing I'll ever do."

---

Then they told me what they'd tried, and this is the part I'd want anybody to hear before they call two nineteen-year-old boys careless.

They'd thrown a line with a weight. Twice. A thrown thing here pushes a wall of air ahead of it and the wall gets there first, and the stone went eleven feet sideways, and it took them two days to find it again.

They'd made a net on a pole. Same problem, slower.

They'd built a kite, which was a good idea, because kites are the one technology that world is genuinely rich in. But a kite drags a wake behind it like a boat and the wake shoved the stone forty feet and down-plain, and they lost it for five days and Gil didn't eat for two of them.

They'd climbed the nearest tower and waited for the drift to bring it past, and a man on a tower top displaces enough air just by being there, and by reaching, and it slid away from Gil's fingers by about the length of my arm.

That's the one he talks about. He was closer to it that day than anybody has been since it left his hand, and what pushed it away was him.

They'd asked the village to build. The village said no, kindly, and went on feeding them.

"They think we're mad," Robin said. "Not in a cruel way. They think it's a religious thing. They've got a word for it and it's the same word they use for a man who won't cut his hair."

---

We ate with them that night, which is a sentence that ought to have more in it.

They fed two strange boys who fell out of the sky for a month without ever once discussing it in front of them. Badly at first, then properly, once the boys made themselves useful, and the boys made themselves useful within about four days because two riggers from a city of riggers had turned up on a world of riggers and it turns out knots travel.

Robin can splice. He can splice better than anybody there, because Sill splices four strand and they splice three, and that is genuinely all it took.

There's no such thing as a hearth there, so there's no such thing as sitting round one. What there is, is a fire pit with a bellows line: eight people in shifts working leather bags to keep air moving under it, because a fire that isn't being fanned goes out in four seconds. They cook for the whole village at once, twice a day, and everybody works the bags at some point in their life, and the bellows line is where the gossip happens the way it happens at a well.

Dray took a turn on the bags. Nobody asked him to. He worked two bags at once for an hour and the woman next to him laughed until she had to sit down, and that was Semm, who ran the cut and the build crew and about half of everything else, and who I would come to rely on more than anybody on that world.

Later I got Gil on his own at the foot of the tower and put it to him plainly, because I'd been carrying it around all day and it wasn't getting lighter.

"You know how this ends."

He didn't look up.

"I want to hear you say it, because if you've got it wrong I'd rather find out now."

"You go when your stone goes," Gil said. "Not when you want to. Not tonight, not tomorrow, whenever it does it. And if I've got a hand on you when it does, I go with you, and so does Robin, and that's it. That's the whole of it. We don't have to get up the cliff and we don't have to do anything clever. We just have to be touching you."

"That's it."

"I've known that since about four hours after we landed," he said. "I worked it out sitting under the cliff. I've had thirty-one days to think about a man walking in here and holding his arm out."

"And?"

"And I'm not going to take it," he said. "Not if it goes before the twelfth."

The haze had gone a deeper gold with the light dropping, which is the closest that world gets to evening, and up above us the thing was turning over and catching and going out.

I let it sit for a while. It's an easy thing to say in a warm room and a different thing to say standing under it.

"That's my father," he said.

"That's a stone."

"That's the one they wrapped him in." He said it without heat. He'd clearly had this argument with himself more times than I'd had it with anybody. "There's a house in the Four Hundreds with nothing in it. There's a chair he sat in and a coat that got sold and a grave with nothing under it, because there was nothing under it, because he came home as a man in a sheet and we buried him and the only thing that came back with him was that."

"And your mother gave it to you."

"She put it in my hand," Gil said, "and she said it would keep me honest, and I have thought about what that word means every single day for thirty-one days."

I didn't say anything for a bit.

"So I'm going to go and stand in my mother's kitchen," he said, "and I'm going to tell her that I couldn't afford two stones so I risked the one thing she had left of him, and I lost it in the sky of a world with no name, and then I came home. Or I'm going to go and stand in my mother's kitchen and give it back to her."

"You understand that if it goes early I can take your wrist myself. You'd not stop me. Nobody your size ever has."

He looked at me then, and he was nineteen, and it didn't help.

"Would you?" he said.

---

I've done it. That's the thing. I did it eleven months ago to a man who'd gone soft in the head on a world with one color in it, and I got a rope on him and he fought me and I took him out of there, and I was right, and I'd do it again tomorrow.

The difference is a thin one and I've never managed to write it down properly.

Ord couldn't choose. That's the whole of it. There wasn't enough left in him to have a view, and where there's no view I'll make the decision and take what comes.

Gil Coombe had a view. He'd had thirty-one days to build it and he could say it out loud in order without crying, and there is no version of my code that makes it my business to overrule a man who can do that.

Nobody gets left. That's the rule and it's not a philosophy, it's a condition, like a limp.

It has never once meant nobody gets a say.

"No," I said. "I won't."

He let out a breath he'd been holding since I said Nell's name.

"Then how long have we got?"

"Eleven days on the other two," I said. "Yours went clear on the eleventh, didn't it."

Robin's voice came out of the dark behind me, because Robin had been sitting there the whole time and I hadn't heard him, which is the only time in eleven days that anybody got near me without me knowing.

"Eleventh day," he said. "Just before what passes for noon. We watched it go pale up there over about four days and then it went clear and it's been clear ever since, and I wrote it down, and I've been sitting on it for three weeks and there's nobody to tell."

"You've measured the window on an unwalked gate."

"Have I?"

"Two hundred crowns," I said. "That's a bounty. That's yours."

There was a silence.

"Two hundred crowns," Robin said, in a very small voice, "is more than my father makes in three years."

---

So the plan was Robin's book and the rest was arithmetic.

Don't chase it. Everything they'd done for a month had been chasing it, and every single attempt had shoved it further out, and the reason was always the same: to reach for it you have to move, and moving moves the air, and the air is the thing that holds it.

So you don't reach for it. You work out where it's going to be, and you get there first, and you're already there and still when it arrives.

The ring takes eight days. Robin had it coming back across a stretch of open plain south of the village on the morning of the thirty-ninth day, at a height of between twenty-eight and thirty-two feet, moving at about the pace of a slow walk.

Thirty-ninth day since they arrived. Which was the twelfth of mine.

I sat with both watches out and did it four times and got the same answer four times, and the answer was that on the morning Robin's stone came home, Sparrow's clock and Dray's clock would have run out the day before.

---

There was one more thing to buy and I had to buy it from the village.

A tower takes a crew. Forty feet of drilled block, guyed in eight, cut new, built where nothing is, on open powder, in four days.

They said no to two boys they liked. They were never going to say yes to a stranger for the sake of a superstition.

So I put my line knife on the mat.

It's a Corrigan hook knife, eleven years old, and I've cut a man off a fouled rig with it and cut my own harness twice, and there is no object on that world remotely like it and everybody in that circle knew what it was within about half a second.

Then Dray put down his spike driver, which was worse, because he'd had it longer.

Then Sparrow, who has nothing, and who I had entirely failed to watch, unpicked the four steel rings off her own harness and put them down in a row, and one of the old men made a noise.

They built it in three.

---

# CHAPTER FOUR

Dray put a block into the sky on the first morning.

I'd better explain what Dray is first, because he doesn't say enough for anybody to work it out on their own. He's Cas's, and he's the quietest man in the trade and the strongest, and every problem he has ever met has been solved by putting his back into it. That's not a criticism. It's a career, and it's a good one, and it has never once let him down.

They were bringing block up from the cut and stacking it, and a block on that world weighs about what a folded newspaper weighs here, and Dray had eleven of them in his arms and was doing fine. Then one started to go, and he did what any man in Sill would do, which was throw a hand under it and shove it back up.

It went up eighty feet and kept going.

Not fast. That's the thing. He had time to reach after it, and time to take a step, and time to stop, and then the whole build crew stopped, and about thirty people stood in the powder with their heads back and watched a stone block go up into the gold at the speed of a rising bubble.

Nobody laughed. That was the worst part. On any site in Sill somebody would have laughed.

Dray put his hands down at his sides and stood there and went a color I've never seen on him.

Then Semm came over and took him by the wrist and showed him, twice, the way you show a child, which is that you don't push. You put your hand where the block is going and let it come to you.

"You are all like this," she said to me, without any heat in it at all. "The two boys were like this. You come down off the wall and you push everything. Where is it you are from that a thing has to be pushed?"

I told her, roughly, and she thought about it.

"Then everything there is trying to get to the ground," she said. "That is a strange way to have to live," and she went back to the cut.

He was better than any of them by the end of the second day. Once he understood that the job was steering rather than lifting, he could carry twenty blocks up a lattice while a local was still tying his cord, and he worked eleven hours a day without a word and I had to make him stop twice.

That's what he was there for and I hadn't known it when I hired him. I'd thought I was buying strength. What I was buying was a man who can put in eleven hours and doesn't need to be talked to.

I paid four times the day rate for quiet. It is the best money I have ever spent and Cas Lorde is never going to hear that from me.

---

On the seventh day I made the mistake of looking up.

Not at the stone. Higher, past the big gauzy things going along in the top of the gold minding their own business. There were smaller ones up there, a long way higher, dark, not moving the way the big ones move. Just going along level in a slow line, and there were more of them than I'd expect of any bird.

I'd been on that world seven days and had been carefully not asking.

"Robin. What's up there?"

He didn't look up. He'd had longer than me to not look up.

"People," he said. "The ones nobody caught."

He finished the bearing he was writing and then put the charcoal down, which for Robin is a considerable gesture.

"They weight their dead. That's what the collars are, the stone rings round the graves that aren't graves, because they can't dig, so a grave here is a bundle with rock lashed to it and it costs a fortune and everybody pays it. And if you can't pay, or if you go off a boom in the dark and nobody's near enough, then you just go." He picked the charcoal back up. "There's a word for them. It means the ones still traveling. They say it kindly. I've heard mothers say it to children about a grandfather and there's nothing in it but fondness."

I looked back down at a chip of clear glass turning over thirty feet above a plain, on a world where the only thing that has ever gone up and stayed up is a person, and I understood for the first time what the village thought we were doing.

They built the tower anyway.

---

Sparrow called the clock.

That was her job by then and she'd made it hers on the second day. Every morning at what passed for first light she'd hold her stone up and read it and say the number out loud, and the first three times it was funny, because she's sixteen and she announced it like a town crier.

It stopped being funny at about day six.

Under a gold sun everything in the indigo range reads as brown, and mine reads as brown at the best of times and lies about it, so the only honest calendar on that world was in the fist of a girl who'd been out four times in her life. She checks her stone every few minutes because she's new. The whole trade sneers at that. It was the most useful thing any of us had.

"Violet," she'd say. "Proper violet now. Not the dark one."

Day nine she said it and didn't do the voice.

---

I told them on the evening of day nine, sitting on cut block at the foot of a tower that was thirty-four feet up and needed to be forty.

"You're going home tomorrow," I said to Sparrow. "And you're taking Robin with you."

They both started at once. Robin got further, because Sparrow stopped to plan.

"The stone comes past on the twelfth. Your clock's done on the eleventh, probably in the morning, and I'd rather have a day of margin than be arguing about it in the dark. You've got a stone and it'll carry a passenger, and Robin's going to be the passenger, and that's decided."

"I'm not leaving him," Robin said.

"You're not leaving him. You're getting off this world so that when something goes wrong out here there's one boy in it instead of two, and that's not the same sentence and I'd like you to stop hearing it as if it were."

"Then we tether," Sparrow said. "All of us. Rope."

"To what?"

She opened her mouth and shut it.

"Say it," I said, "because you've got it right and I want to hear you say it."

"Skin for people," she said. "Attachment for things."

"So if I hold your rope when it goes."

"Nothing. It goes clear, you're standing in a field holding a rope, and I'm on the Salt." She was furious and she was crying and she was doing neither of them where the village could see. "It's stupid. It's the stupidest rule there is."

"It's the only rule there is," I said. "Everything else is technique."

Robin said, "What if you're still here on the twentieth?"

"Then Gil comes home on my arm on the twentieth with the stone in his fist, and you'll have been standing at that gate for nine days feeling sick, and that's the good version."

"And the other one?"

"You know the other one," I said. "You've written more of that world down than the Order has. You tell me."

He didn't. I've since decided that was kindness rather than obedience, and it took me longer than it should have to work that out.

---

I've thought about whether to write down what I said next and I'm going to, because I have a horrible feeling it's the truest thing in this account.

"You're not being sent home because you're sixteen," I said. "You're being sent home because you're carrying the Order's stone and it's got sixty crossings written on it by a man who was guessing, and if it embers out here with you on it then there is nothing coming for you, and I'll be standing next to you when it happens and there will not be one thing I can do."

"You'd give me yours."

"I can't. Mine's under my skin."

"Then Dray's."

"Dray's is going home on Dray's arm and Dray is a grown man with a sister on Blackrail." I got up. "Go home. I'll meet you there."

"You always say that."

"I've never said it to you once," I said, "and I'd like a chance to have said it and been right, because I'm running short of those."

---

It knocked at what passed for noon on the eleventh.

She was sitting on a block with her boots off and she came up off it like she'd been bitten, and said, "It's going, it's going," and then got hold of herself and said it again properly.

Two minutes. That's what everybody gets and it's never once felt like two minutes to me.

We stood in the open in a ring facing inward, which is what crews do on every world there is, and she had it flat against the inside of her wrist and her other hand out for Robin, and half the village came at a run and then stopped at a distance because Semm put her arm out.

Robin took her cuff.

I knocked his hand off it hard enough to hurt him.

"Skin," I said. "Not cloth. A bare wrist. If there isn't one you take a neck or an ankle and if there's nothing bare you make some, and if you've got hold of a sleeve when it goes you stay here and she doesn't and there is nothing anybody can do about it afterwards."

He went the color of the powder and got her bare wrist and held it in both hands, and Sparrow counted out loud from there, in Sill, because you count in whatever you learned it in.

Then he did a thing I hadn't expected. He got Robin's book out from inside his jacket, one-handed, and pushed it into Gil's chest, and said, "Twelfth. It's all in the back four pages and I've done the sums twice and the second time I did them backwards."

Gil couldn't take it. Gil had both hands over his mouth.

I took it off him.

She got to eighty-eight and they were gone.

There was a noise the air makes closing up, and a fine bloom of powder went up where her boots had been and hung there, and the cord she'd toggled to the post went slack and drifted like weed.

Semm asked me afterwards how long the two minutes had been going on for. Not how it worked. How long it had been the way it is. I said three thousand years that anybody knows of, and she said something to the woman next to her that I didn't get, and neither of them looked at me again for a while.

The village had not been warned about any of this and I have not stopped feeling bad about that either. About forty people had come to watch what they thought was a farewell. Three of them sat down.

---

Dray's knocked four hours later. He had the whole two minutes and he used about six seconds of it, and what he said was, "Right," and that was all, and I've known him eleven years.

Then there were two of us on that plain, and a tower thirty-nine feet high, and one clock nobody could read.

---

We put the last two courses on by the light of the second morning, and that night Gil asked me the question I'd been waiting two days for.

"How long have you got?"

"Six days. Seven."

"How do you know?"

"Size is capacity," I said. "The door hands out a window and a stone holds as much of it as it can hold. An ordinary one held eleven days here, and mine's the size of a pocket watch where everybody else's is a thumbnail, so mine holds a good deal more than eleven. That's not a guess, that's how they work."

He nodded and went to sleep, and I sat up.

All of that is true. What I left out is that mine is cracked, and cracked means it runs backward and skips and clears early, and twenty years of notebooks and two watches have never found the pattern in it. I could see the shade and the shade was brown and brown means nothing.

Six days. I have no idea where I got it from. I have thought about that number a great deal since, and what I have decided is that I chose the smallest lie that would let a boy sleep, and that if I'd said two days he'd have taken my wrist and I'd have had to let him.

---

The last night he talked.

He'd said almost nothing for a day and a half and then it came out all at once, the way it does when there's one other person and no lamp and nothing to do until morning.

"She'll be at the door," he said. "That's the thing I keep getting to. She'll hear the gate and she'll be at the door before I'm up the step, because she's done that nine times a month for eleven years and it's never been anybody yet."

"That's not a bad thing to be walking toward."

"I've got two of them," he said. "Two of what happens next. In one of them I've got it in my hand and I don't say anything, I just hold it out, and she takes it." He was quiet for a bit. "I've had that one a lot. It's not as good as you'd think. In that one she's got the stone and she hasn't got a son who can be trusted with anything, and I've watched her work that out about four hundred times now."

"And the other?"

"The other's the true one. In the other one I haven't got it, and I say so, and she puts the kettle on." He laughed, badly. "That's what she'd do. That's what she did when they brought Dad's boots back. She put the kettle on and she said we'd manage and she was right, we did manage, and I have never once in my life been able to stand it."

I didn't say anything. There isn't a thing you can say to that and I've had nineteen years to find one.

"Nell'll be worse," he said. "Nell will want to know exactly what I did, in order, and she'll listen to all of it and then she'll say it wasn't my fault, and she'll mean it, and she'll be wrong."

"It wasn't."

"It was mine and it was in my hand and I put my hand out." He turned over. "I'd rather you didn't do that. I've had a month of the village doing that. They're very kind about it and every single one of them thinks I'm a fool, and they're not wrong either, and I'd sooner have the one man here who's said a true thing to me keep doing it."

So I said the true thing.

"You are a fool," I said, "and I'd have done exactly what you did, at nineteen, with two hundred crowns of debt and a friend and one stone, and so would every man I've ever worked with, and half of them are dead. That's not comfort. That's just the trade."

"No," he said. "That'll do, though."

Then he went to sleep like somebody had turned him off, and I sat up with his book and read the back four pages for the ninth time.

---

It came in on Robin's numbers.

He had it at between twenty-eight and thirty-two feet and it came in at twenty-nine. He had it a quarter hour after what passes for sunrise and it was late by about the time it takes to boil a kettle, and he apologized for that, out loud, to me, as though he'd let the side down.

Gil went up at the first sighting, an hour before, and got himself wedged at the top of a forty-foot lattice with a cord round his waist and both bare hands in his lap, and then he did not move.

That's the whole of it. That's the trick, and it took a village three days to build and a boy a month to earn.

He didn't reach. He'd learned in the worst possible way what reaching does. He sat with his hands open and his elbows on the frame and let it come.

The last twenty feet took about four minutes and it drifted straight at his chest like something that had been looking for him, and I stood at the bottom with both watches in my hand and forgot to look at either of them.

He breathed through his fingers at the end. I saw him do it. He turned his head and breathed down into his own cupped hands so the air off his mouth wouldn't get to it.

Then he closed both hands, very slowly, on nothing at all, and had it.

---

He came down in eleven seconds and he was screaming, and it took me a second to understand it wasn't anything bad.

I caught him in the powder, which on that world is easy and doesn't feel like anything, and he went down on his knees anyway and made a noise I'm not going to write.

"Up," I said. "Now."

We stood on open ground with the tower over us. I had his bare wrist in my right hand and my own sleeve up and I could feel his pulse going in three places and I have never in my life been so aware of one object.

Gil had the stone in his fist. Not against skin, in his fist, and it didn't matter, because a thing comes home if it's held and it doesn't care about cloth or skin or anything else. That's the rule that makes this trade possible and it's why people forget that people are different.

People are not luggage. People need skin.

"When it knocks you'll feel it come up my arm," I said. "It's a big one and it's not subtle. Then you count, and you don't let go of me for anything. Not if I fall over. Not if I tell you to."

"Why would you tell me to?"

"I wouldn't," I said. "I'm covering the ground."

Then we stood there.

---

Here's what I hadn't told him and hadn't told Robin and had been carrying on my own since the ninth day.

Mine knocks early. It knocks when it has nothing to say. And once, twenty years ago, in a room, in the middle of a sentence, it didn't knock at all.

So a man with a working stone stands there and waits for two minutes of warning, and I stood there waiting for two minutes of warning that might be a lie, or might be four minutes, or might not come, and the whole of that gold afternoon was me deciding not to explain any of it to a boy holding my wrist.

It knocked twice for nothing. The first time I got his hand up and said the word and then had to stand there and tell him no, and watch what it did to his face, and do nothing about it. The second time I didn't say anything at all and he felt it anyway.

Somewhere in the middle of it I had decided, privately and completely, that it had embered, and I got as far as working out where I'd sleep and whether they'd let me on the bellows line before I made myself stop.

Then it came up my arm like somebody knocking a door in, and Gil said, "That's it, that's it, is that it," and I said yes and got his bare wrist in my hand and we counted.

He got to a hundred and nine in Sill and then the ground was white and cold and it was November.

---

You arrive the way a dropped thing arrives.

We came down on the plinth at 1,714 in the last of the afternoon and both of us went straight over into the salt, because we'd had our weight in the wrong place for twelve days and had forgotten what it was for.

It was so cold it hurt my teeth. The sky was hard and pale and about nine miles deep and after eleven days of gold it was almost unbearable to look at, and the whole world was suddenly full of edges.

Gil lay on his back in the salt with both hands closed on his chest and didn't move at all for about a minute.

Then he opened them and looked, and it was clear, and it was already lightening in his palm from the crossing it had just done, and it was the most ordinary object in the world.

---

Robin got to him first.

He'd been at that gate thirty-one hours. Hollis told me afterwards that he'd been out of the hut and back nine times and hadn't gone into the city and hadn't gone and got warm, and that for the last of it he'd stood on the salt with his hands in his armpits watching an arch, having done the only piece of real science of his life and having no way at all to find out whether it had worked.

Neither of them said anything. Gil got the fist out and opened it in front of his face and that was the whole conversation.

Sparrow hit me in the chest with the flat of her hand, once, hard, and then went and got the blankets, and did the rest of it with her back to me.

Dray was inside with the stove going and four cups out. Four, not five. He'd been that sure and I have never once been that sure about anything.

Hollis came out with the book and the lamp and I said, "Two out," and stopped, and had to start again.

"Three out," I said. "Five back."

He wrote it. He had to turn the page to do it, because the two names from the fourth of last month were the last two entries in six years and there wasn't room under them.

Then he stood there in the cold with the book open and looked at it.

"That's the first time," he said.

---

I filed the account eight days later.

Air, water, ground, the towers, the nets, the bellows line, the toggles and the loops, and the nine or ten people I could describe well enough to be useful.

The window: eleven days, measured twice, filed in Robin Tarr's name, and the two hundred paid to his father's house in the Four Hundreds, and I'd give a fair amount to have seen that.

Nineteen hundred for the account, which is the largest single figure of my working life, and I would like to walk anybody who cares through what happened to it. The rental against the account. Fourteen days of Dray at four times the day rate. A Corrigan hook knife, a spike driver, and four harness rings, replaced out of my own money and not itemized to anybody. What came out the far end of that was enough to be comfortable on for a year. I have spent it. I could not tell you on what. Cas got her hour first, in a room with no copyist, and read it twice and asked four questions and every single one of them was about the towers, because Cas has never in her life been interested in anything except what a thing can be built into.

The Order's rental came back with sixty written on it, less one, so fifty-nine now, on the paper, as a fact.

And I gave them everything.

Except one sentence.

---

Linen keeps a room off the Ninth Market with no lamp in it, which suits both of us.

Face and hands entirely wrapped, heavy robe. He has been to more worlds than any living person and has never filed one of them, which makes him the only archive in existence that isn't the Order's, and he doesn't take money.

"Sexton."

"I've been somewhere new."

"1,714. Eleven days. The account is filed and Cassandra Lorde read it on Tuesday." The wrappings moved. "None of that is worth anything. It is in a book now."

"I held something back."

The room got very quiet.

"That is a serious thing to say out loud," Linen said. "In this city that is a criminal thing to say out loud."

"I know what it is."

I sat down on the crate he keeps for the purpose.

"On that world," I said, "nothing that leaves your hand comes back to it."

I let that sit there.

"A dropped thing doesn't fall, it drifts. A thrown thing pushes the air ahead of it and moves what you were aiming at. A flame smothers in its own smoke. A hole fills itself. Nobody there can dig, so nobody there has ever mined anything, so there is no metal, so an entire civilization runs on knots. And the only thing on that world that has ever gone up and stayed up is people."

Nothing moved for a long moment.

"That is not in the account."

"No."

"That is the whole of the account," Linen said. "The rest is furniture."

"Yes."

"You have committed the one crime this city takes seriously in order to walk in here holding it." A pause. "Which means you already know what is being asked for in exchange, and have known for some days, and have come anyway."

"Twenty years ago," I said, "a body came back through Gate 807 with something of my sister's in the wrappings, and I logged the body and I didn't log the object."

I put my hands flat on my knees, which is a thing I do when I've decided.

"That's the trade," I said. "That sentence about the air, against what's past the Eight."

Linen was still for a very long time.

"Come back on Thursday," he said. "There is a great deal to say and none of it can be said quickly, and one part of it should not be said at all."

---

I went out through the Ninth with the roof gone opaque with salt over my head and four hundred worlds' worth of unidentifiable objects sorted by nothing but size, and I got about eleven steps.

Then I stopped in the middle of the aisle like a fool, with people going round me on both sides.

Because I'd just heard a man who has never once in his life said the word *I* tell me there was something he wasn't going to say.
