# THE TEN THOUSAND: STORY ONE, THE RED

---

> *From The Traveler's Primer, printed at Sill, one crown.*
>
> A traveler who dies abroad is sometimes returned by the people among whom he died. The Order receives all such returns without charge and examines them, and the examination is not an indignity. It is the only means by which the living learn anything of the place. Why some peoples return a stranger's body at their own great cost, and others do not, is not known and has never been usefully studied.

---

# CHAPTER ONE

The Order doesn't like to waste a trip.

A man goes out through a door, dies somewhere nobody's ever been, and comes home wrapped in a stranger's cloth. That's a journey. Somebody paid for it with their life and somebody else paid for it with a stone they'll never get back. The Order's position is that the least you can do is find out what the trip was worth.

So they put him on a slab in the receiving hall and they pay me two crowns to take his clothes off and tell them everything I can.

Two crowns. Two. For a man who came home in a stranger's cloth with half a world in his pockets. I have raised this. There is a form for raising it. I have filled that form in four times, and on the fourth occasion a clerk explained to me, kindly, slowly, in the voice you'd use to explain a stair to a dog, that the fee reflects the labor and not the finding. Fark him and the ledger he rode in on. May his stone clear at the bottom of a well. I hope he lives to ninety and spends every day of it indoors, in a small warm room, with a man who wants to talk about his knees.

The clerk on the slab that morning wasn't that clerk. This one's called Pell and I have never once called him that. In my head he's been the Damp Boy for nineteen years, because he sweats when he writes and he writes constantly, and at this point I couldn't tell you which one started it.

"Boots," I said.

He got them off. Cheap Sill boots, resoled twice. The second resole wasn't Sill work. Wooden pegs in a double row instead of nails, and the leather wasn't from any animal I've met.

"He was out a long while," I said. "Long enough to wear through, long enough to find somebody who could fix them. That's six months at the least. Write down that there's a cobbler out there. Somebody with a trade and a shop and time to do it properly."

The Damp Boy wrote.

I got the shirt open. There was a burn across the collarbone, healed badly, and the pattern of it was a grid.

"That's a live rail," I said. "He got across a powered line. So they've got current out there, and they've got it strung where a man can walk into it, and your file says nobody's home. Your file's wrong."

"The file is four hundred years old."

"Then it's been wrong for four hundred years."

He wrote that down too, which I'll give him. He didn't enjoy it and he wrote it anyway. The Damp Boy will write anything. You could tell him the man died of being insufficiently loved and he'd ask you how to spell it.

Then I looked at the wrapping.

They'd bound him properly. Chest to hip, even turns, arms tucked in, a folded pad over the sternum so the stone wouldn't shift on the way. An hour's work by somebody who wasn't in a hurry.

Which meant that wherever he'd died, the people there had taken the one thing they owned that could have sent one of their own children home, and tied it into a stranger's chest, and carried him to the door, and let go.

I've been looking at that for nineteen years. It doesn't get easier and I don't let it show.

"Put down that they're honest people," I said. "That's worth more to you than the boots."

---

Ansel's boy found me on the steps.

"There's a gentleman at the Lazaret asking after you," he said. "Ansel says to tell you he came in a carriage."

Nobody comes to the Lazaret in a carriage. The Lazaret sits nine miles out at the edge of the desert where the rail line stops, and everybody who goes there goes on the train with everybody else, because that's what the train is for.

A carriage means you don't want to be seen on the train. Which means everybody sees the carriage.

"Did he order anything?"

"Tea."

I stood there for a second.

"Tea," I said.

"Ansel gave him one. He asked for another and she said no."

The Lazaret serves beer, spirits, and four things brewed on the far side that I wouldn't touch. It has tea because Ansel keeps a tin behind the taps for people who've come out of a door in a bad way and can't hold anything else down. That tin was old when I was young. I've looked in it. Whatever's living in there now has been in there long enough to have opinions.

Ordering tea across that bar at four in the afternoon marks you the way a top hat marks you.

So he came nine miles in a private carriage, sat down in a room full of working crews, asked out loud for the one thing in the building nobody orders, drank a cup of hot silt, said thank you for it, and asked for another. Ansel said no. That was the kindest thing anybody did for that man all week, and she got the best afternoon of her year out of it.

He'd been in the room ten minutes. Every crew in there knew he was somebody's clerk and knew he was hiring.

---

His name was Fisk.

Good coat. Not a great one. There's salt in the hem from thirty feet of walking between the carriage and the door and he kept touching it, like it might come out.

It doesn't come out. Salt is in the mortar and the bread and the inside of my left boot. There's a man in Low Ninety who has kept himself in a house and a wife for twenty years selling a preparation that lifts it out of cloth, which it does not do, and which he sells to about forty men a year in good coats.

He had a folder. He opened it, squared it, and turned a page round to face me. People do that when they want to look like they're hiding nothing.

A likeness, badly drawn. Nathaniel Ord. Age thirty-four. Gate 660. A date. Whoever did the drawing had been told what a face is and had gone at it from there.

The date was nine months old.

"He went out on a day contract," Fisk said. "He was to meet a party on the far side and conclude an exchange. The party never arrived. Mr. Ord did not return."

"And you've waited nine months."

"We waited the appropriate interval."

"You waited six."

He didn't answer, so I said it for him.

"Sill presumes a man dead at six months out. Before that your company owes his people a recovery and a payment and a written account of what you sent him to do. After it you owe nothing at all. So for six months a rescue was a debt you had to pay, and on the first day of the seventh it turned into a thing you could choose to do if the price was right."

"That is the law," Fisk said. "I did not write it."

"No. But somebody in your building has a calendar."

Somewhere in that building is a man whose entire employment is knowing what day it is. He is pleasant. He offers you a chair. He is better paid than I am and I would like that written down.

---

"Mr. Ord was carrying a case," he said.

There it was.

I'll set the next part out plainly, because it's the whole thing.

He said, "Our authorization is four hundred for the recovery of Mr. Ord and the case."

I said nothing.

He said, "Our authorization is eight hundred for the case."

Behind the door somebody dropped a tray and the bar cheered. Neither of us moved.

"Say that again."

"Eight hundred. For the case."

"On its own."

"For the case."

I picked up his card, turned it over, and put it back down.

"What do you pay for the man on his own?"

Fisk looked at his folder. He straightened a page that was already straight. He didn't look up.

I counted four seconds. A silence can be an accident for two.

"That's all right," I said. "That's an answer."

Four hundred for both. Eight hundred for one. The case is worth more to them arriving without the man who carried it, and there's only one reason for that. A case can't tell anybody where it's been.

"What's in it?"

"I'm not able to tell you."

"Not able, or not allowed?"

"Not able."

I believed that. He said it too quickly and there was something under it. A man who doesn't know what he's fetching has also been told to stop asking.

"Eight hundred," I said. "Half now."

---

I want to be clear about what I was agreeing to, because I've had this argument with myself before and I lose it every time.

I took the money for the case. I was always going to bring the man.

I've got one rule and it isn't a philosophy, it's a condition, like a limp. Nobody gets left. Not clients, not strangers, not people I don't like. If I'm within arm's reach when my stone goes clear, they're coming with me, and I've never once managed to talk myself out of it standing there in the moment, so I've stopped trying to at the desk.

I keep the figures on what that rule has cost me. In a book, at home, in a column, in my own hand. Nineteen years of extra weight, extra crossings, and stone burned down to nothing carrying people who had no way on earth to pay for it, and the total at the bottom of that column would buy a house in Sill with a yard on it. I could stop counting. I have thought seriously about stopping. What I did instead was buy a better book.

Fisk didn't need to know that. He was buying a case. What else came back through the door with me wasn't in his contract, and he'd made very sure of that himself.

He wrote out a bank paper and a gate authority stamped that morning, which meant they'd decided to hire me before they'd met me.

---

Ansel was at the taps. Ansel is always at the taps.

"Well?"

"660."

The riggers at the long table stopped arguing about their knot.

"Nobody's gone out there in years," Ansel said.

"Four. Then Ord, nine months back. Nobody since."

"There's a reason."

"I know."

"Then go and find out what it is properly," she said, "before you go and find out the other way."

---

# CHAPTER TWO

Nobody walks through a door they haven't read on. There are four ways to find out what's on the other side and I used all of them, because the alternative is arriving somewhere and learning why nobody goes.

---

The first is the file.

The Ledger House is an honest stone building wrapped around something that isn't. You go up the steps and through the hall and then the wall changes. On one side, brick and mortar and three hundred years of soot. On the other, a surface with no seam anywhere in it, no join, no wear, cold to the touch, standing there before the city existed. They built the Order around it the way you'd build a case around a jewel.

There's a rope in front of it now and a small painted sign asking visitors not to touch, which is the most hopeful object in this city. Three thousand years of weather couldn't mark that wall. The Order has decided the danger is fingers.

Verity Ash keeps the reading room. She's small and gray and wears the seamless charcoal they all wear, and dust has never settled on her in her life. In nineteen years I have never heard her shorten a word. Not one. I've listened for it the way other men wait on a horse, and I've got nothing, and at this point I'd be sorry to get it.

She had 660 on the desk before I asked, which meant somebody had told her I was coming.

"Nine lines," she said. "You will not enjoy them."

Habitable. Breathable. Water. Vegetation. People, farming, no hostility recorded. Window between nine and thirteen hours, wide variance, cause unknown.

Then the last four lines.

*Illumination is monochromatic. All color on this world reads as a single hue. Anchor stones cannot be read by sight. A constant low tone is present.*

"Nine lines for a habitable world," I said. "That's nothing. A habitable world with water and farms should run to forty pages."

"It should."

"So what happened?"

Verity folded her hands. "Four crews went through after the survey. Three came back early and filed nothing. One did not come back at all."

"And the crews that came back early. Somebody sat where I'm sitting and wrote four lines about the light and the sound and then stopped."

"Yes."

"They knew more than that."

"They knew a great deal more than that," she said. "A crew that comes home four hours into a nine-hour window has seen something and decided what to say about it. What they chose to write down is a warning. It is simply not the kind of warning people recognize, which I suspect was the point."

---

The second way is the gate itself, and the man who sits at it.

There's a clerk at every one of the ten thousand. Not just the busy ones. Every single door in that desert has a hut beside it and a man or a woman in it with a book, and they're there in February and they're there in August, and the Order pays them nine crowns a month and a stove, and nobody has ever robbed one twice.

I say twice because it's happened. About sixty years ago a crew turned over the clerk at a gate in the four hundreds and took what was in the hut. What the Order did about it is not written down anywhere, which is exactly why nobody's tried since. Some things you learn by watching and some things you learn by asking somebody who watched.

The clerk at 660 was a thin woman called Merrow with a stove and a kettle and nine years at that door.

The first thing she did was take me round the front and show me what people had left.

---

Nobody carves anything into a gate. You'd have to be a lunatic to put your knowledge somewhere the weather can get at it and any liar can cut a line through it.

What people do is leave things.

The foot of every gate is heaped with them. Crews come out and they leave what they brought back and couldn't sell, or what they want the next crew to see. Sacks, tools, banners, boards with names burned into them. Somebody had propped a framed picture against the leg of 660 and the frame had rotted to nothing while the picture stayed.

Gate One is buried to the waist. Some of the far ones have nothing at all in front of them, and a bare gate tells you more than a heaped one, because it means nobody's ever come back through with a reason to stop and put something down.

660 had about a knee's worth and most of it was old.

Merrow keeps the important things in the hut, out of the weather, and shows them to anyone going through. That's the job. The book is the job and the hut is the job.

She laid three things on the table.

A pair of goggles with the lenses stove in. Not broken by an impact. Broken by somebody putting a tool through them on purpose.

A coil of good line, unused, still in its shop turns, left by a crew who'd walked out of a hut and gone through and come back four hours later and not wanted it any more.

And a slate, the kind clerks use, with four words on it in chalk gone hard.

*THE NOISE DOESN'T STOP.*

"Who left the slate?" I said.

"Man off the last crew. Nineteen years back." Merrow put the kettle on. "He wrote it, he set it down there, and he went home on the train and I never heard another word about him."

"And the goggles?"

"Same crew. He did that himself, out there, before he came back." She didn't look up. "I've had nine years to think about why a man puts a spike through his own eyeglasses on a world where you can't see color, and I've got nothing that makes it better."

---

Then she did the rest of the job, which is the part people forget the clerks are for.

She took my name and Veth's. She took the contract number and the gate authority. She asked who to notify if we didn't come back, and I said nobody, and Veth said there was a tailor, and she wrote *tailor* in the book without any expression at all.

And she went down her list of standing bounties.

"660," she said. "The Order will pay for a filed account of the sound. Cause, source, whether it varies. Forty crowns."

There's a form for the account of a sound. I've filled it in twice. Section four asks you to describe what you heard. Section five asks you to describe what you heard, in your own words, as though section four had been written by somebody else and you were only now being consulted.

"Forty's low."

"660's got a survey and a population and nobody's died on it in four years." She turned the page round so I could see. "That's the low end. This is the other end."

The other end was a column of gate numbers with nothing written beside them. No survey line, no population, no window. Just a number and a figure.

Some of the figures were four hundred. Some were a thousand. One was more than I've made in my life.

"Never opened," Merrow said. "Two hundred and forty of them inside the rail. Nobody's ever walked through any one of those doors."

"And past the rail?"

"Not on my list."

"Why not?"

"Not on my list," she said again, in exactly the same voice, and turned the page back.

I've had that answer out of the Order maybe nine times in my life. It's always those words and it's always that voice, and the part that stays with me isn't the refusing. It's that every one of them has been taught the same sentence.

"How many go?" I said.

"Every year a few. Usually the young ones, usually in summer." She took the book back. "Nine years I've been here. I've written four names into that column and I've never once got to write a second line under any of them."

---

The third way costs money.

There was a deepwalker in the Lazaret that night, three days off a run, and I bought him four drinks before I got to it. Lazaret prices. I put it on the sheet as inquiries. Fisk's company will query it, and I will win, because I have won this argument with better companies than his and I have kept the letters.

Deepwalkers go past one door. That's the whole trade. Your stone binds to the gate you came through and it brings you back to that gate, so ordinary crews go in one door and come out the same one. A deepwalker carries their stone off the skin, in a boot or a tin on a cord, and lets it go clear while it isn't touching them. Nothing happens when it does. The stone just goes clear and dead in the tin, and now it's free to bind again, so they walk to the next gate on that world and cross, and it binds there instead.

One door deeper. Then again.

They can go four or five doors out. They're also the only people who can't come home in a hurry, because their stone brings them back to whichever door it last bound to, and that door is deep. To get out they have to walk the whole route backward, gate by gate, in order, with a fresh clock on every leg. That's why they carry a knotted cord with a knot for every door they've passed, and why losing the cord kills you slowly instead of quickly.

They're the best in the trade and nobody trusts a word they say, because a deepwalker who finds a rich patch wants everybody else too frightened to go near it.

This one had never been to 660. He knew a man who had.

"Went in on a nine-hour window and came out at four," he said. "Wouldn't tell anybody why."

"What did he say?"

"Nothing. That's what I'm telling you. He came back and he never got right again." He turned his cup round. "Went home, sat in his rooms, wouldn't have a lamp lit. His sister looked after him about a year and then he died in there, and it wasn't the trip that killed him, because the trip was four hours."

"Four hours."

"Four hours and he never got over it. That's the whole of what I know and I'd want more than four drinks to say it in front of his family."

He also told me about a place four doors past a gate in the six hundreds where the houses are all standing and the food is on the tables and there's nobody in any of them, and it's bigger this year than last.

I paid for the drink and didn't write that down. Deepwalkers lie for money.

---

The fourth way is your stone, and mine is no help to anybody.

Mine is welded into my left forearm under a coin of burn scar. It's the size of a pocket watch, where every other stone in the world is the size of a thumbnail, and I've never met or heard of another one like it. It's cracked, and a cracked stone runs backward sometimes, and skips shades, and clears early, and once refused to bind at all for eleven days.

It can't be read and it can't come out and it can't be assayed. That's been the arrangement for twenty years.

Which is why Veth carries his own.

I own a second stone. Ordinary, clear, bought over four years with money that should have been rent. I can never use it, because the gates won't let anybody carry two, and mine is under my skin and staying there. So the only thing that stone is good for is going on somebody else's body.

I could tether Veth to me and bring him home on mine. People do that. But then Veth's life is a thing I'm holding, and if my stone does what my stone does, he goes wherever I go and has no say in it. With his own stone he's got his own clock, running to the same gate, and if we're separated out there he still comes home.

His is honest. Mine isn't. That's the whole reason.

I've never had his assayed either, because the man who reads wear works for people I'd rather not sit down with. So neither of us knows how many crossings are left in it. It might be forty. It might be one.

I told him that in the Ninth Market while a tailor let out a coat for the fourth time this year.

"You always say this," Veth said.

"It's always true."

---

I should explain what Veth is, because people get it wrong and then get hurt being wrong.

He's not a wizard. His people built their laws into their own bodies, generations back, the way this city builds a clock into a case. He can take a local condition and hold it off. Make water stop being water. Make a sound stop being a sound. Four yards across, ten seconds, maybe twelve.

And then there's less of it, and there's no way to get more, because the door to his world has never been found. What he has is what he arrived with, and it's been going down for thirty years. You can see the level. There's a light under the skin of his throat and it's dimmer every year.

He's seven feet of elongated courtesy in Sill formal dress that has never fitted him and never will. Four arms, which the tailors have made their peace with. A pale smooth face with a scatter of small dark eyes set into it, none of them where you expect.

And here's why he comes with me.

Somewhere out there is a world with the material his people used to charge themselves. He doesn't know which one. He doesn't know what it looks like. He's fairly sure he'd know it if he stood in it.

So he's been through forty-one doors in thirty years, and every single time, the first thing he does on arrival is stand very still for about a minute with all his arms down and find out whether anything's different.

Nothing ever is.

He can't buy his way through a door. Stones cost a year's wages and companies won't spend a companion's place on a man who costs more than he carries. I'm the only person in this city who'll take him through, and the price is that when I need ten seconds he gives me ten seconds, and it comes out of a supply that will not be refilled.

He does the arithmetic every time. He's never once said no. I've never once pretended that's the same as it being free.

"660," I said.

"A single color and a sound that will not stop." He picked the stone up in two long fingers, held it against the light, which does nothing and which everybody does, and put it in his waistcoat. "Then I shall want a great deal of tea when we return."

Veth is the reason Ansel keeps that tin. He has been drinking out of it for thirty years, he calls it a comfort, and I have never had the heart to look him in any of his eyes and tell him what I saw in there.

---

# CHAPTER THREE

The Salt starts where the rail stops caring.

You come off at the last platform and there's nothing in front of you but white. White ground, white sky, and the two of them meet in a haze so there's no horizon to speak of.

Then you see the first arch, and then you see all of them.

They stand in a line running east until they're too small to count. Every one is identical. Same height, same width, same pale unweathered stone, same tall narrow opening, same round disc set above it that nobody has ever explained. Whoever cut them didn't vary once.

The openings aren't open. Each one holds a flat pale sheet like standing water seen edge-on. It doesn't ripple and it doesn't show you anything back.

The scale is what gets you. A mile off, the near arch is enormous and the far ones are teeth, and they're still in a perfectly straight line, and they're still going.

Everybody says ten thousand. Nobody has counted. The rail runs out a little past two thousand and everything the trade has ever done, every survey and contract and bounty anyone in this city has collected, happened inside those. Past the rail you walk, and it's salt all the way with no water in it, and the line keeps going into the haze and doesn't thin and doesn't curve.

And every single one of them, as far as anyone has ever gone, has a hut beside it with a light in it and somebody sitting in it with a book.

Which means the Order's been out to the end and knows the number.

Somewhere in the Ledger House is a clerk who orders the lamp oil. He knows it exactly. He is the only man in Sill who does, he is paid what a clerk is paid, and I have spent real hours of my life imagining that conversation. It never ends with him telling me. In the better ones he at least lets me sit down.

They've never said it. I've heard four men ask in my life. They all got the same sentence.

---

Merrow walked us out to the plinth.

You put your stone against your skin. Not through cloth. Cloth isn't touching, and the difference between touching and nearly touching is your life.

I put my palm flat over my forearm. Veth took his out of his waistcoat and pressed it into the inside of one wrist, and used a second hand to check my rope, and a third to hold his hat on, and kept the fourth free out of habit.

Then you take hold of whatever's coming with you and you check each other, because you bring what you're holding and nothing else.

Then you walk into the sheet.

There's no curtain and no resistance. It's the exact feeling of stepping off a curb you didn't know was there. Your foot goes down further than you expected, one time, and by the time you've finished flinching you're somewhere else, and the light is wrong, and the air is a different temperature.

Behind you there's an arch identical to the one you left. If you walk back through it you haven't gone home. You've only spent a crossing.

The stone against your skin goes dark. Deep indigo, nearly black.

That's your clock starting.

---

At 660 it went dark and I couldn't see it.

The whole world was red.

Not tinted. One color, and only one. Red ground, red rock, red scrub, red water in a channel by our boots, red sky with a sun sitting in it like a coal. Everything out there is a shade, lighter or darker, and that's all your eye is getting.

I held my arm up. Red.

Veth held his stone up. Red.

"Ah," he said.

"Yes."

So I got my watches out instead. Brass on the right, Sill-made. On the left the found one, running on something nobody can identify. Thirty minutes apart, which for them is close.

I wrote the time on my cuff in pencil.

That's the whole method. Two watches that disagree, a pencil, and twenty years of not knowing how long I've got.

Four different collectors have offered me money for the found one. Every one of them asked what it runs on. I told all four the same thing, which is that if I knew what it ran on I'd be selling that instead and they could come and see me about the watch in a year.

Veth stood still for about a minute with all four arms down, the way he does.

"No," he said, eventually, to nobody.

"No?"

"No."

---

Ninety seconds after that I heard the Note.

You don't hear it when you arrive. Your ears are busy with the fact of being somewhere else. Then it's there.

Low. Continuous. Under the range where you could tell somebody what it was. It doesn't come from a direction and it doesn't get louder toward anything and it doesn't vary.

I caught myself holding my breath to hear past it. There's no past it.

Six words in a four-hundred-year-old file.

---

I gave us six hours out of somewhere between nine and thirteen.

Then we started looking, and here's what I actually do. It isn't clever. Anybody could learn it in eleven years if they were willing to spend eleven years doing it.

I spent eleven years taking the clothes off dead strangers.

Bodies come back from every world there is and somebody has to unpack them and write down what they were carrying. Boots, buttons, thread, pegs, tar, the way a seam's finished, the way a knot's tied. I've handled Sill work from four hundred years and the other kind from everywhere else, and I can tell them apart in bad light without thinking.

Nathaniel Ord went through that door in a good Sill suit and nine months of clothing.

He went through with no money.

So he sold it.

---

We found the first piece inside two hours, in a hamlet of six houses upstream, on a woman's wrist.

A button. Bone, four holes, turned on a lathe, with the shank finish they do on Cutter Row and nowhere else on this world or any other.

She had it on a cord with eleven other things and she was pleased I'd noticed.

"Where'd you get it?"

She answered in a version of Sill about nine generations rotted. "Traded up-valley. Long back."

---

The lining turned up two hours after that. Somebody had made a bag out of it. Good gray Sill twill with the maker's chain-stitch still in the seam, worn through at one corner from months of carrying.

"Up-valley," the man said, before I asked.

By late afternoon we had a shirt cuff, a boot heel with Sill nails in it, and half a waistcoat cut down for a child.

Each one further in, and each one from deeper inside the suit, because he'd sold the outside first and worked his way in. I was walking backward along nine months of a man taking himself apart to eat.

Veth got it around the boot heel.

"He is selling himself in order."

"Least useful first."

"Then the nearer we come, the less of the suit there will be."

"Yes."

"And when there is none of it left?"

"Then we've found him."

---

The settlement was forty houses of red brick in a bend of the channel with terraces going up the slope behind, and about two hundred people.

They saw us a long way off. Nobody ran and nobody armed themselves. Two men came out to the edge and waited with their hands where we could see them, and one of them made a sign at me.

It's the hand-sign for the Return. Somebody taught it to hundreds of worlds that have never met each other and every one of them still uses it. He was telling me that if I died there, they'd tie a stone to me and carry me to the door.

I gave it back.

"We're looking for a man who came through a while ago," I said.

They looked at each other, and then one of them smiled like I'd said something lucky.

"You've come for the Oracle."

---

They took us up through the terraces and everybody came out and the children ran alongside, and it was friendly, and I didn't like a second of it.

There's a way people look at you when they're taking you to see something they're proud of. I've had that look off a man about a well, off a woman about a nephew, and off a fitter in the Ninth Market about a device that has never once been made to do anything.

"Emmet," Veth said, in his own language, which he only uses when he doesn't want to be understood. "They are not taking us to a prisoner."

"No."

"To what, then?"

"Something they own."

---

He was in a house at the top with the door taken off, and there were eleven people sitting outside on the ground waiting their turn.

Nathaniel Ord. Age thirty-four. Nine months in.

He was wearing the last of the suit, and I want that written down properly, because it's the whole story in one look. No lining. No buttons. No boots, his feet wrapped in cloth. What was left was the shape of a respectable man with nothing respectable inside it.

Hair to his shoulders. Beard to his chest.

On a cord round his neck, an ember. A stone that failed partway through a return and froze there, still faintly lit, permanently. A cheap stone bought by a cheap company for a one-day contract. It carried him twice and quit on the third.

And on a folded cloth in front of him, both hands flat on the lid, a black case about the size of a hat box.

A woman was asking him about her son's chest.

"The green under it," Ord said. "Under and under, that's where it sits, that's the wet in the corner and the corner's got a name, hasn't it, it's got a name and the name is the damp coming up, so you take him off the floor. Off the floor. Off it. And the case says the same, the case has said it twice now, so you take him off the floor."

The woman thanked him and gave him bread and went away looking better than she came.

Which is the thing. He's not wrong. Get a wheezing child off a damp floor and the child improves.

He's been doing that for nine months, one answer in every eleven landing, and two hundred people who have never had anything else have built the rest out of hope.

---

He looked up and saw me. His eyes went over my face, then down to my bare left arm, then to the stone burned into it.

He stopped talking.

"Oh," he said. "That's a big one."

"Mr. Ord."

"That's a very big one. That's the biggest I've seen and I've seen a good many." He stopped. Started again. "You're from the city."

"I'm from Sill. I've come to take you home."

Everybody outside went quiet.

"Home," Ord said.

"Yes."

"How long?"

"Nine months."

He laughed. It went on a bit long.

"No," he said, kindly. "No, no. Years. It's been years, I've been here years and years, ask them, ask anybody. There was a suit. Do you remember the suit? I had a very good suit." He looked down at himself with real puzzlement. "I don't know where the rest of it went."

"Mr. Ord, I need the case."

Both hands came flat down on the lid and every person in that doorway moved half a step in.

"That's not. No. No. That's where it comes from."

"Where what comes from?"

"The knowing," he said, and he was patient with me, the way you're patient with somebody slow. "It talks. Not out loud, I'm not mad, it doesn't talk out loud. It's got everything in it. Everything anyone ever wrote down, all of it, all the wisdom of the world, and it comes up through my hands when they ask me things, and if it's not here then they've got nobody, have they. They've got nobody at all."

He looked round at the eleven people sitting on the ground.

"They need me," he said. "Nobody ever needed me before."

---

# CHAPTER FOUR

We sat on a wall outside in the last of the light, which there is just a slightly different red.

"He will not give it up," Veth said.

"No."

"And they will not permit it." He had two hands on his knees and a third holding his hat against a wind that had come up. "Emmet, they are not fanatics. That is what troubles me. They are kind, they are bored, and he is the only thing that has ever happened here."

That was it exactly. Nine generations in a bend of a channel under one color with a sound in the air, and then a man falls out of the sky who answers questions and gets it right about one time in eleven.

They weren't keeping a god. They were keeping a doctor and a priest and a piece of theater, and they were going to fight for him, and they'd be right to.

I got both watches out. Five hours forty on the brass, five ten on the other. Somewhere between three and seven hours left and no way to narrow it.

"I need ten seconds," I said.

Veth was quiet for a moment.

"You have been building toward that since the button."

"Since the file."

"Yes." He looked at his hands, all four of them. "You understand that there is not another. Not on this trip. Not on the next one."

"I know what it costs."

"You do not," he said, "but you are polite about it."

---

We went in an hour before dawn, which there only means fewer people are standing up.

Ord was awake. Ord's always awake. That's what the Note does.

He was sitting exactly where he'd been sitting nine hours earlier, both hands on the lid, and when my shadow came across the doorway he spoke without turning.

"You can't have it."

"I know."

"Then why have you come back?"

"For you," I said.

And I got the tether round him.

I did it the way I've done it four hundred times, one loop under both arms and the running end through my own harness ring, pulled up short so there's no slack in it at all. He can't get out of that. Nobody can get out of that without a knife and time, and he had neither.

He came off the floor screaming.

He wasn't a starved man of thirty-four. He was nine months of one color and one sound, and he got a thumb in my eye and his teeth into my forearm above the stone. Behind him the doorway filled with people who'd been kind to us all evening.

"Gary."

"Yes."

"Now."

---

Nobody ever sees Veth do it.

There was a smell like hot stone.

And the Note stopped.

I need to get this right. It didn't go quiet. Quiet is something you can have anywhere. A sound I'd been carrying in the middle of my head for nineteen hours was simply gone, out to about four yards, and what came into the space where it had been was so big that I forgot for a second what I was holding.

Ord stopped fighting.

He stood up straight in the rope and his hands went to his own face and he looked round that room at all those people with his mouth open.

"Oh," he said.

Then, "I'm still here."

And he cried. Not the way a man breaks down. The way a man cries when the pain stops, which is with surprise.

Ten seconds. Maybe twelve.

The light under Veth's throat went right down and he sat back against the doorframe with two arms on the ground holding him up.

And the Note came back.

It came back all at once. I watched it land on him and I watched what it took away with it.

I took the case out from under his hands while he was still gone. He didn't notice for about four steps.

Then he did, and he started screaming for it, and he was tied to me and he came anyway.

---

They came after us down the terraces and this time nobody got out of the way.

I put a spike through the meat of a man's shoulder with the bolt-driver at four feet, left-handed, badly, and he sat down and stayed down. Somebody put a stone into Veth's back. We got out of the settlement and about a mile down the channel and then it was two hundred people behind us in the dark and three of us in front, and one of the three was tied to me and fighting every step, and one was gray at the throat and running like a gentleman, which is to say badly.

The gate was two hours south.

We didn't have two hours' worth of anything.

I got my arm up. Red. Useless.

Brass watch said eleven and a half hours. The other said eleven. The file says nine to thirteen.

So we were somewhere inside the window and there was no way on this earth to know where.

"Veth."

"Yes."

"Stone. Now. On the skin."

"We are not at the gate."

"We don't go to the gate," I said. "The gate comes to us. Put it on your skin and keep it there and don't let go of it whatever happens."

He got it out of his waistcoat with one hand and clamped it against the inside of his wrist with another and kept running with the other two, which is a thing only he can do.

I put my palm flat over my forearm and got my other fist in Ord's collar with the skin against his neck, because you bring what you're touching and cloth isn't touching.

And we ran south in the red dark with two hundred people behind us and no idea at all whether we had four minutes or four hours.

Ord was howling for the case.

The first of them got close enough that I could hear him breathing.

And the world went away.

---

You arrive the way a dropped thing arrives.

We came down on the plinth at 660 with the sky just going, and I went onto one knee in the white with a case locked under one arm and a man on a rope.

Cold. Real cold, February cold. And after nineteen hours of one color the salt was so completely, stupidly white that it hurt to look at.

And silence. Actual silence, nine miles from anything.

Veth landed four feet away on his own stone, on his own clock, which is exactly why he carries one.

I lay there for a second and got my arm up in front of my face. Clear.

Twenty years and I can count on one hand the times it's done what it was supposed to do at the moment it was supposed to do it. That was one of them. I'd like to say I'd trusted it. I hadn't. I'd just run out of other plans.

---

Ord made a sound I'm not going to write down.

Then he got up onto his hands and knees in the rope and looked at the black sky and the white ground.

"It's got colors," he said.

"Yes."

"It's got colors in it."

I cut him loose.

---

I opened the case on the salt, in the dark, before he was finished looking at the sky.

I'd told myself I wouldn't. A man who hasn't looked can't be made to say what he saw, and that's real protection, and I gave it up. But I'd just watched somebody lose nine months and most of his mind to that box and I was not going to hand it over without knowing what it was worth.

The lock was nothing. Two turns with a spike.

Inside, one sheet of paper.

A list. Eleven names, each with a gate number beside it and a date beside that. Handwritten, in a clerk's hand, and ruled off neatly at the bottom as though the list was finished.

Nathaniel Ord was the ninth.

His gate and his date were already written down before he ever went through it.

I read it twice. Then I put it back exactly as I'd found it, and closed the case, and turned the lock over.

---

He was sitting up by then, looking at the arch and the huts and the line of doors going east.

"That was in there?" he said.

"Paper."

"What did it say?"

"Nothing you want."

He thought about that. Something moved across his face that was nearly the man in the badly drawn likeness, and then it went again.

"Take it away," he said.

"There's a rail head nine miles east," I said. "There'll be a cart at first light and the driver owes me. Sill's forty minutes after that."

He was already backing away.

I let him. My rule is nobody gets left, and he wasn't left. He was nine miles from a city with both feet under him and nobody from his company standing at the gate to shake his hand.

He went east into the dark at a run, in the rags of a good suit, with a dead stone round his neck.

Merrow came out of the hut with the book and the lamp, and I stood up and moved so the lamp was on me and the dark was behind me, and by the time she got the light up there was nothing out there to see.

"Two out," I said.

"Two back," she said, and wrote it, and that was that. If she'd seen him she'd have had to write him, and once a name is in that book it's in it forever and anybody can ask for the page.

She looked at Veth, who was sitting on the plinth with all four arms down.

"He all right?"

"He'll want tea."

"I've got tea," she said.

---

Fisk received me in a room with a carpet.

Not a hallway and a bench. A room, with a carpet, and a second chair that nobody sat in the whole time I was there. I have been paid in worse rooms by better men.

I put the case on the desk. It sat there being black and heavier than it looked.

He put one hand flat on the lid for a second, which I didn't care for, and called somebody in to take it away without opening it. He never asked whether I had.

"And Mr. Ord?"

"Mr. Ord wasn't in the contract."

He looked at me. I let him.

"Eight hundred, then."

"Eight hundred."

He wrote it out and didn't argue and didn't ask twice, because to Tesk and Halloway this had always been a line item with two prices on it, and I'd picked the one that cost them more and troubled them less.

I put the four drinks in on a separate sheet, itemized, with the deepwalker's trade and the hour. He passed it without reading it. I'd had the argument ready since the Lazaret and he took the whole thing off me for nothing, and I want it understood that I was owed that argument.

"A pleasure," he said, which it wasn't.

---

So they paid me double for bringing back the box and not the man.

They paid the higher figure because a man who comes home can talk.

Nathaniel Ord is in this city now, broke and thin, in the last of a good suit, with nine months of a red sun in his head and a dead stone round his neck and a very clear memory of a meeting that never happened.

He can talk. He will, when he's able. Men do.

And there were eleven names on that list and his was the ninth, and I don't know what the list is for, and I know two things about it that Fisk doesn't know I know.

I know it exists.

And I know somebody wrote Ord's gate and Ord's date down before he ever walked through the door.

---

Ansel put a mug in front of me I hadn't ordered.

"Well?"

"Eight hundred."

She stopped wiping. "For a box."

"For a box."

"And the man?"

"Somewhere in the market."

Ansel looked up from the taps. Second time this year. It took the whole room down half a step in volume, which she noticed and didn't care about.

"Huh," she said, and went back to the taps. "Drink that."

Veth sat down beside me the careful way he's been sitting down all week, and put two hands flat on the bar and used a third to straighten a cuff that ends two inches too high.

"I have been thinking about the sound," he said.

"Don't."

"I have been thinking about it a great deal."

"Gary."

"That is not my name," he said comfortably, and picked up his tea with a hand that wasn't quite steady.
