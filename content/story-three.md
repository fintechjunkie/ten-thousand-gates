# THE TEN THOUSAND: STORY THREE, THE EIGHT

---

> *From The Traveler's Primer, printed at Sill, one crown.*
>
> Doors are numbered in the order they were found and not in the order they lie. A low number means only that somebody reached it early. What governs the order in which one door leads to another is not known. Travelers who take the numbering for a measure of distance, difficulty, or safety have done so before and are not, as a class, still traveling.

---

# CHAPTER ONE

I gave Linen my sister's knife on a Thursday in January and went back for it eleven days later.

The first Thursday was the one he'd named at the end of the gold job, and I'd walked in there expecting to be told a route and got told to go home and fetch something.

"Talk is worthless," he said. "Bring the object."

So I went and got it out of a drawer where it had been for nine years, and I put it on the crate he keeps for the purpose, and I have never in my life found it harder to take my hand off something.

---

I'd better say what the knife is.

Nine years ago a body came home through Gate 807 and I was on receiving. It was a deepwalker, and there was no cord in the wrappings, which means somebody out there cut it off him before they wrapped him or he'd already lost it.

There was a knife.

Corrigan hook knife, four-strand pattern, the old one she stopped making before the fire. Two letters filed into the tang with a needle file, done badly on purpose so they couldn't be polished out.

A. B.

My sister took that knife out through 807 twenty years ago and it came back through 807 nine years ago on a dead stranger, and I logged that body and every single thing on it and I did not log the knife.

That's the crime if anybody wants it. I've never pretended otherwise.

---

I'd looked at it myself for nine years and got exactly this far: four repairs, none of them Sill.

That's the end of what I've got. I can tell Sill work from the other kind in bad light without thinking. I cannot tell you which world, because that needs somebody who has stood on the ground the material comes from, and I've been through one door in twenty years.

There is one man in this city who has stood on that much ground.

He isn't of the Order and never has been. He has been to more worlds than any living person and has never filed a single one of them, which is the whole point of him: he is the only archive in existence that isn't theirs. They've asked. He said no, and they left it, and nobody I know can tell me why they left it.

He doesn't take money. He takes facts, and he doesn't write anything down, and telling him a thing is the one way in this city to make it not the Order's.

---

Eleven days is what it took, and it wasn't reading. It was matching.

He keeps a room behind the first room and I've never been in it. What's in there is objects. Forty years of them, traded for one at a time, and he had to go through them by hand and by memory and find things that were the same.

I asked, once, how many.

"Enough," he said.

That is the longest answer I have ever had out of him about anything, and I have been going into that room for nine years.

---

He had it on the crate with a cloth under it and he did the newest work first, which I hadn't expected, because that's my own method and I'd assumed it was mine.

You find a man by starting with the outermost thing he sold. Coat first, boots last. So you go backwards, and the layers give you the order.

"The pin through the tang," Linen said. "Last work. Not iron."

"What then?"

"Dark. Cold-worked. Harder than it should be." A pause. "There is a spiral punched into the head. Three turns, off center."

"Decoration?"

"No. Every made thing on that world carries it. Underside, same place, punched before the piece is finished."

"Have you been?"

"No."

"Then how do you know where it's from?"

"That is not for sale."

---

I sat there for a second.

"Say what it gets me," I said.

"A team stands on that ground and picks up a cup and turns it over. One hour. Less."

"And that's the fourth world."

"That is the fourth."

---

"Under the pin. Older. The haft has been replaced." He moved the cloth about an inch. "Fire-hardened. There is black glass ground into the grip where a hand goes."

"Ground in deliberately."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Grip."

He waited, and I understood after a moment that he was waiting for me to ask the useful question rather than the interesting one.

"What's the world like?"

"The ground is warm. Not everywhere. Warm underfoot, and in places more than warm." A pause. "Nothing sleeps on the floor there. Every bed is up off it. The glass is in the soil in quantity."

"So a field looks like what?"

"Like somebody has sown it with broken bottles."

"That's the third world. I've stood on that one."

"You were carried off it."

---

"Older again. Underneath the haft, so it was there first. A lashing. Reed, split flat, plaited in fours."

"Reed."

"It grows standing in water. It is worked wet."

"And?"

"White sky. The water does not drain. There is no dry ground and has not been for a long time. Everything is built up on stone causeways."

"Who built the causeways?"

"Not them."

"How will they know they're there?"

"Wet boots," Linen said. "Four minutes."

---

So that's the route. I want it set down plainly because everything else in this account is about what it costs.

807. Known. Everybody's known it three thousand years. Salt marsh, cold, and a population that hasn't wanted to talk to us since before my grandfather.

Two. White sky. Standing water. Stone causeways nobody living made.

Three. Warm ground. Black glass in the soil. Nothing sleeps on the floor.

Four. A spiral punch on the underside of every made thing.

Four worlds. Four doors. The first one is the only one anybody in Sill has ever seen.

---

"How far did she get," I said.

"Past the fourth."

"You can't know that."

"There is wear on the pin." The wrappings shifted. "It is not the wear of a knife carried round a village."

I sat with that for a while.

"So somebody was walking."

"Somebody was walking."

---

Then he gave me the bad half, and he gave it to me flat, and I'd like it on the record that he didn't have to.

"None of it tells anybody which door."

"Say it again."

"Four hundred doors on a world. They are identical." A pause. "Knowing the second world has a white sky does not tell a man which arch on the far side of 807 opens onto it. That is walked, not reasoned. Weeks a leg."

So here's what I actually bought, and I only worked it out on the stairs afterwards.

I didn't buy a route. I bought the ability to know when somebody has arrived.

Before this, a man could have stood on the second world and had no way at all of telling it from the eleven other worlds he'd tried, because the only description anybody had was that Ada Bell had walked through a door.

Eleven men over eleven years. I've spent that whole time assuming they failed.

One or two of them may have got there and come home and told me nothing, because there was nothing to tell.

---

He kept the knife.

I want that down, because I walked out of there without it and I have not had it in my hand since, and it is the only object in the world that my sister carried and I can prove it.

He kept it because he has to. Every knot those crews bring me gets held up against that knife, and there is one man alive who can do that, and the test has to live where the man is. I worked all of that out on the stairs and it did not help at all.

---

There's one more thing and it's the thing I've thought about most.

At the end of the first Thursday, before any of this, he told me there was a great deal to say and that one part of it should not be said at all.

Eleven days later he said all of the rest, in order, without being asked twice.

He never went back to that part. I didn't ask. I'm not sure why not, and I've had a while to work it out, and the best I've got is that a man who has never once in his life said the word *I* had told me there was something he was choosing not to say, and I decided I'd rather owe him than know.

---

I've never described the accident and I'm not going to now, but I'll put down the parts that bear on the job.

Ada was twenty-three and better at this than I was. That's not a thing a brother says to be kind. She read ground faster and she never once walked into a place she hadn't sat and looked at for ten minutes first.

We went out through 807 and we went three doors and we did it properly.

We did it the way it's done, which is the thing that turns people's stomachs and which I want on the page. A deepwalker carries his stone off the skin, in a tin or a boot. He feels it knock in there, muffled, and he sits still and lets the two minutes go past, and nothing happens, because nothing is touching it. Then the stone is clear and free and dead, and he walks to the next door on that world and crosses, and it binds there instead.

One door deeper, with a clean clock and a new anchor.

Everybody in this city is raised to hear the knock as the sound of their life ending in a hundred and ten seconds. We sat through one every few days as method. I did that for eleven years and I have never once been able to explain to a rigger why it isn't obscene, because it is.

On the third world my stone was bound at the third gate. Then something went wrong, and it was fast.

---

The pouch on my wrist was destroyed and the stone went into the meat of my forearm and stayed there, and whatever did that to the pouch cracked the stone on its way in.

That part matters and people get it wrong. They assume I cracked it later with the iron. I did not.

Stones do not break. They do not chip, scratch, cut or crush by any means this city has. Fire does nothing. Presses do nothing. It has been done five or six times in three thousand years and every one of them was an accident.

The Ninety-One Crater is in the northeast field. Four hundred yards of salt fused into green glass with the stumps of a gate in the middle of it. Ninety years ago, so there are people alive in Sill who heard it go and felt the ground move under them at a distance of sixty miles.

That's what a broken stone does. Everything in it comes out at once.

So a cracked one is not a curiosity, it's an unexploded shell, and this city treats it that way. They get weighted and dropped past the shelf, or buried under lime with a slab on top and an entry in the parish book, and the men who do that work are paid a great deal and don't discuss it in bars.

There is one walking around this city in a coat, and it is mine.

It sits in my left forearm with a round patch of burn scar over the top of it about the size of a crown piece. It cannot come out. No surgeon in Sill will touch it, and the one who explained why to me did it kindly and only once.

That is the entire reason rooms rearrange when I come into them, and why two landlords in this city have turned my money down, and why I am always served first everywhere I go. It isn't what I've done. It's what I'm carrying, and every one of them is right, and I have never once argued about it.

I have also never queued at the Ninth Market. Not once, in twenty years. I would like to be able to tell you that I have never used that, and what I can tell you instead is that I keep my left sleeve buttoned everywhere except the Ninth Market.

The blood was coming out of me at a rate I could see and I was going to be dead long before that stone cleared. So I cauterized it in the field, because that's what you do, and the heat welded the scar down over the top. That's the only part of this the iron did.

---

Then it took me. No knock, nothing. Her hands were on my arm and then the light changed.

I came out at the third gate, on the near side, which is the second world.

And that's where my memory of my own life stops for something between five and seven weeks.

---

What a man in that position needs is not medicine.

He is two doors deep with a cord on his belt, and that cord is the only object in existence that knows the way out. One knot per door in order, knot style recording which gate. The way home is not a direction. There is no direction. Every world has doors of its own and there are plenty of them and they are identical, and the one that starts home looks exactly like the forty that don't.

So an unconscious man with a cord can be walked home by anybody who can read it.

An unconscious man without one is furniture.

---

Somebody walked me out. All the way. Three worlds and into the Salt and onto a cart and into a healer's room off Cadge Street, and I was in fever from the arm for the whole of it.

I have four or five pictures from those weeks with nothing joining them and I'm fairly sure two of them aren't real.

I know they were good, because carrying an unconscious man through gates is a nightmare. Every crossing re-binds his stone to the door he just came through. So they had a man on a stretcher with a clock nobody could read and a gate behind them that owned him, and if it had fired at any point they'd have had to go back and get him. Somebody sat down with that problem and solved it three times, for a stranger, over five weeks.

And their price was everything on me. Kit, boots, watch, the coins in my coat, and the cord.

I've never been angry about that and it makes people uncomfortable when I say so. They carried a dying man across three worlds and charged him for it and the invoice was itemized. Cas would call that clean and Cas would be right.

But they took the cord. So what I came home with was 807, which I've known since I was fifteen because everybody knows 807, and nothing else at all.

Two doors, walked twice, in a fever, gone.

The healer was a woman called Dunnet, and she died nine years ago, and all she could ever tell me was that three people carried me in and did not give a name and one of them had a face painted white with a black diamond over the eye, which I spent nine years chasing before somebody told me that's a uniform half the freight trade wears.

---

I can't walk this.

I want to be precise about why, because the trade assumes it's about depth and it isn't. Depth costs nothing. Your stone doesn't care how far in you go.

It's the anchor.

A deepwalker gets a clean clock at every door because he re-anchors at every door, and re-anchoring means letting the stone go clear while nothing is touching it. Mine cannot go clear off my skin, because mine is under my skin. It anchors once, at the door I walk through, and that is the anchor for the whole trip.

So my one window would have to cover the whole of it. Four doors out. Weeks of walking on each world looking for the next arch. Whatever the work turns out to be. Then all of it again backwards on foot.

Nothing that size exists.

And I couldn't read it anyway. It runs backward, it skips, it clears early, it clears late, and it does not knock when it should and knocks when it has nothing to say. Any number I put on my own window is a number I made up.

So I'd be pulled out of the middle of it on a day nobody could plan for. That isn't a man you build a route around. That's a hazard you leave at home.

One door and back. That's my ceiling, it's permanent, and I've known it for twenty years, and it has never once been as bad as it was walking out of that room with four worlds in my head.

---

I've paid for this eleven times. Eleven men, one at a time, over eleven years, on money I got by taking work I shouldn't have taken.

Every one of them was a hire. Wages, kit, a rate for the days.

Which is why it never worked. A man on a rate has a reason to come home and no reason on earth to go further than the rate covers, and every one of the eleven brought me back an account that was exactly as good as what I'd paid for.

Three of them didn't come home. I have those three in a notebook and I've never been able to decide what I owe their people, so I've paid it every year and never said what it was for.

So this time I wasn't going to hire anybody. This time I was going to post a price.

---

# CHAPTER TWO

There are maybe five pairs of deepwalkers working out of Sill in any given year. Some years four. It has never once been more than six in my lifetime.

That's not a guild and it isn't secrecy. It's that the work is filthy and slow, the ceiling is four or five doors, the trade would sooner drink with a body-snatcher, and there is no version of it that makes you rich enough to stop. So the people who last are the ones who can't do anything else, and there are never many of those.

Two of those pairs would take a job like this. The rest are salvage men who go one door deep and come back with sacks, and they'd have listened politely and gone home.

They work in twos.

Two means somebody is awake while you sleep and somebody can carry you. Two can go quietly, and going quietly is most of the job, because you arrive on somebody else's world with no papers and no explanation and every one of them has a word for people like you. Six boots in an alley is a delegation. Two is a pair of strangers who were somewhere else by the time anybody decided what to do about it.

And two means two cords.

That's the real reason. Lose your cord and you're standing in front of the answer with a working stone, forty doors, and no way to know which one starts home. It isn't death. It's worse in a specific way: you keep the stone, you keep the legs, and you never see your own city again as long as you live. So they keep two, written independently, checked against each other every night, and each of them carries the other's life in a knotted string.

I sent to both pairs on the same day for the same hour and did not tell either that the other was coming.

---

They took the back room at the Lazaret, which is neutral ground by statute, and Ansel put a jug down without looking at anybody and went away.

I paid for a notary, which I have never done in my life.

She's a machine, about my height, cream and blue-green and worn, with a rabbit's head and one ear that's been broken and mended at the fold. She keeps a slate and writes with a finger, and she is called Tally, and every serious transaction in the Ninth Market goes through her because nothing she has written down has ever been successfully disputed.

She cannot go through doors. She runs on a dead stone, the way a few of them here do, and a gate will not accept anybody carrying a second one, and hers is inside her and can't come out. So she has spent nineteen years being the most reliable witness in a trade she can never enter, and if that bothers her she has never told me about it.

I hired her for one morning and it cost me eleven crowns. Weasel pays her forty for a day and she has never given a discount to anybody in her life. I have watched a grown man in that market argue a rate with a machine that cannot speak, and lose, and have the losing written down for him.

---

Quillon got there first and was standing when the others came in, which was deliberate, and everybody in the room clocked it.

He's very tall and very thin and his face comes to a point, with four long whiskers off it that move when he talks, and he talks constantly. Teal coat to the ankle, cut for somebody with different shoulders. A hat with a feather in it the color of a bad sunset. I have called him the Peacock in my head for nine years and I have never once said it out loud, partly out of manners and mostly because he would be delighted. He carries a blade that came through a door and lights itself, and everybody who counts things knows roughly how many draws are left in it, and the number is small.

Tice came in behind him and stood with his back to the wall.

Tice is the one people underestimate, and they underestimate him because of the clothes. Blue tunic with a lightning flash stitched on the chest. Stiff white collar standing off the shoulders like a small cape. White boots to the knee. Two little brass rods off one shoulder that used to do something and have not done it in forty years. I asked him once what they had been for. He thought about it and said they were for the shoulder.

He looks like a man in a shop window and he has buried more people than I have. He came through a door forty years ago from a world that had got further than this one in every direction and then stopped, and he has never once told anybody which door.

The gun on his hip has nine shots in it. Not nine in the magazine. Nine left in existence. He's carried it thirty years and fired it twice, and both times everybody present understood that he'd spent something he could never get back to kill that specific person.

Ceryl came in without any of that.

Bone-pale, white hair down past the waist, a long dark coat with a crescent standing up off the collar behind her head. It isn't part of her. It's a made thing and she has never explained it to anybody in this city and nobody has asked twice. She has a sword at her hand that carries its own light, and she has drawn it in this city twice that I know of, and it has never cut anybody here.

Four doors and back. Twice. Nobody else in the trade can say it and nobody disputes it, and she has never explained how, which is the single most irritating fact in the profession.

Toller was behind her, in black, and where his face should be there's a lantern of moving light in a cracked case. He had a length of chain over one arm with a brass bell on it.

The bell is why anybody deals with them. It sounds as loud or as soft as he wants, it's heard at any distance and through any substance, and nobody has ever managed to stop it. Which means those two can separate on a world where nothing carries, a mile apart in haze or dark, and be back inside the two minutes.

No other crew in this world can afford to split up. Those two can. It's why a pair of them covers ground that ought to take five.

---

They started on each other before I'd finished pouring.

"Fifty a day and kit," Quillon said. "Retainer of four hundred. A route like that is six weeks minimum and I would rather be paid for six weeks than argue about it in a year."

"Three hundred," Ceryl said.

"Three hundred." He turned his whole body towards her, which he does, because it makes the feather move. "Ask her what she thinks the third leg is. Go on. Say it out loud in front of the man paying."

"Nine days across."

"Nine days across warm ground where the water goes bad in four. She knows that. She said nine anyway, because she wants the number small in front of a client, and I want to be fair to her, that is not lying. That is optimism. Optimism on the third leg of a four-door route is how a person becomes an entry in somebody's book."

"You have never stood on it."

"No. Neither have you. That is my point and it is the only honest thing anybody has said this morning."

"Two hundred and fifty," Ceryl said.

Toller hadn't moved. Tice hadn't moved. Tally wrote all of it down and I could hear her doing it.

I let it run, because everything either of them said about that route was worth more than the beer, and because two deepwalkers undercutting each other in a room is the only circumstance in which either of them will tell you a true thing about ground they want.

Ansel charged me for the jug, and for the room, and for a second jug that nobody had asked for and that arrived at precisely the point in the morning when a second jug was going to be useful. She was right about all three. I paid it without looking at it, and she noticed that too.

Then I said, "There's no retainer."

---

The room went a particular way. Not angry. Insulted, which is more useful, because an insulted man tells you what he actually costs.

"Then we are finished," Quillon said, "and I would like it noted that I came out in weather."

"Sit down. I haven't told you the number yet."

Tally stopped writing.

That's the thing about her. She doesn't emote and she doesn't editorialize and she stopped writing, and every person in that room understood exactly what it meant.

---

"Three prices, all on delivery," I said. "You take nothing from me on the day you walk out of this room.

"Five hundred crowns a knot.

"Five thousand for proof of life.

"Twenty thousand for return."

Nobody said anything.

"A knot," Ceryl said.

"A cord I can put in front of the man who read that knife. First knot's 807 and that's free, everybody's had that for three thousand years. Second knot is five hundred. Third is five hundred. Fourth is five hundred. And it has to survive him."

"Meaning what."

"Meaning he'll tell me whether the world at the end of it has a white sky and standing water, or warm ground with black glass in it, or a spiral punched into the underside of every made thing on it. If it doesn't match, you've found a world, and I'll shake your hand, and I won't pay you."

"Fifteen hundred for three doors," Quillon said. "Do you know what three doors costs?"

"Yes."

"Then you know it is insulting."

"It's meant to be thin," I said. "The road isn't what I'm buying. What's at the end of it is."

---

"Twenty thousand," Tice said. It was the first thing he'd said. His voice is flat and the vowels are wrong in a way forty years hasn't fixed.

"Twenty thousand."

"The Order pays nineteen hundred for a door nobody has ever opened."

"I know what the Order pays."

"So you are telling four people in a bar that one woman on mapped ground is worth ten unwalked gates."

"I'm telling you what I'll pay."

Quillon sat down. He did it slowly and made a performance of it and he still did it, and Ceryl had never stood up, and that was the moment those two stopped competing on price and started competing on something else.

---

"Proof," Ceryl said.

"There's a tattoo on her face. Not small, not common. There is nobody else in the world with it."

"Describe it."

"No."

She waited. She's good at waiting.

"Anybody who's seen her can describe it to me," I said. "That's the test and it can't be worked round. Bring me a name and a village and no mark and I'll pay you for the knots and thank you and that'll be the end of it. Bring me the mark and I'll know inside four words."

"And if she is dead."

"Then you tell me she's dead and how you know, and I'll pay the five thousand, because proof is proof. She's alive or she isn't. Either way I'd know, and knowing is the thing I'm buying."

---

Then Quillon asked it, and I'd known it was coming since the day I decided to do this, and six days hadn't produced a better answer.

"Have you got it?"

"Which."

"The twenty thousand." He wasn't performing, and his face without the performance is a much older face. "I will walk four doors for a man who has it. I will not walk four doors for a man who does not, and I would rather be told here, in front of witnesses, than in a year with a woman's mark in my mouth."

Everybody in the room was looking at me. Tally had her hand up over the slate.

"I do," I said.

---

I didn't.

I only had eight hundred and forty crowns.

I'd had nineteen hundred in November for the best account of my career. Then the Order took two hundred back against the rental. I paid Cas four times a day rate for eleven days of a very large man. I paid Corrigan for a hook knife I gave away on another world and for ninety feet of line I did not bring back. I paid a boy's father what I thought his son's numbers were worth, which was more than the Order thought.

And then I paid the three, which is the part nobody knows about and which I have never once written down.

Three of the eleven men I sent out looking for Ada did not come home. I have their names in a notebook. I have never been able to work out what I owe the people they left, so what I do is pay it every December, in cash, at the door, and I have never told any of the three houses what it is for and not one of them has ever asked me twice.

That is where nineteen hundred crowns went in nine weeks, and if you want to know why a man who works constantly has nothing, that is the whole of the answer and there isn't a better one.

So: eight hundred and forty crowns, one cracked stone in my arm, one clear one in a tin that came home on Dray's arm out of the gold, and twenty thousand of debt that four people had just watched me take on.

---

Tally wrote it down. All of it. The three prices, the test, the date, the room.

Then she turned the slate round to show me, and she'd done what she does, which is put the full name in.

*Emmet Bell, of Sill.*

Nobody calls me that. The trade calls me Sexton, off eleven years of body detail. People who've never met me say the Gray Man. There are men in bars who say Two-Clocks, and the stranded, when they write to me, write to the Rope. The Order has me down as Eleven, which was a shift number before it was a name.

Emmet Bell is what my mother called me and what my sister called me, and there are perhaps four people left alive who use it, and one of them is a machine with a mended ear who was being careful.

I looked at it for a second longer than I meant to.

"Correct?" she said.

"Correct."

So as of eleven o'clock on a Thursday morning, in the back room of the Lazaret, there was a written instrument in this city in a hand that has never been successfully disputed, in which Emmet Bell of Sill undertook to pay twenty thousand crowns.

It's the largest lie I've ever told. I told it in four syllables, and nobody in that room believed me except the two who needed to.

---

# CHAPTER THREE

Weasel is enormous and slow and immaculate and he prices everything, and I went to him because he's the only man in Sill who'd answer the question without laughing. There were four other people I could have asked. All four would have laughed, three of them would have told somebody, and two of those three would have had it to Cas Lorde before I got home and taken my coat off.

"How does a man raise twenty thousand crowns."

"He does not."

"Say the rest."

"There is no rest." He moved one ring a quarter turn. "Forty years I have sat in this chair. Nobody in this city has ever raised twenty thousand crowns by working. It is not a large sum, Sexton, it is a different kind of sum. It is a warehouse. It is a rail contract. It is what a family has after four generations of not being stupid."

"And the ones who get it in one go?"

"They sold something they should not have had." He put his hands flat. "That is not advice and it is not a moral position. It is the only route and everybody who has taken it went to prison or went to the Order, and in your case those are the same building."

That is the only free advice Weasel has given anybody in forty years, and he gave it to me because it cost him nothing to give. On the way out I checked whether he had put it on my account. He had not. I checked.

---

Cas sent for me on the Tuesday, which meant she'd known since Thursday and had spent four days deciding what to charge.

She had the ledger out and wrote nothing in it, which is how she thinks.

"Not the route," she said.

"I hadn't offered it."

"You came in braced to refuse me the route. I am telling you I do not want it. Four developed worlds with people and weather on them and nothing on any of them that comes home in a sack. There is no salvage in a farm."

"There's a survey."

"There is a survey I would have to pay to have walked, four doors deep, over eight months, to sell to the Order at nineteen hundred a door if the Order would take an unverified deep account, which it will not." She looked up. "I run a business. I am not interested in your sister and I would like that on the table so you stop flinching."

"Then what."

"I am offering you eight thousand crowns as an advance against work."

---

I want the terms down exactly, because I've thought about them a great deal since and I don't want the version I keep at home.

"Nine unwalked gates inside the rail," Cas said. "I have the bounties reserved on all nine, which cost me money, and they expire. I cannot walk them because the people I would send are twenty-two years old and they die. I lost two crews in four years and I have stopped pretending that is bad luck."

"You want me to lead them."

"I want you to lead them and teach them. Eight thousand now. You work it off a gate at a time, and if a gate produces something real the balance clears early and you keep a share. I would guess three years. It could be two."

"And in those three years."

"You are mine," she said. "Not a partner. Not a contractor. You take the gates I choose, in the order I choose, with the crews I put in front of you, and if I need you on the eleventh of a month you are there on the eleventh of a month." She capped the pen. "It is honest work, it is the best offer you will receive in your life, and it would keep four or five children alive who are otherwise going to die in front of you anyway."

That last part was not a manipulation. That's the trouble with her. It was a fact she happened to be holding.

"No," I said.

"Give me the reason and give me the real one."

"I've never had a boss and I'm never going to have one."

"That is vanity."

"Yes," I said. "It is. It's the only thing I own outright and I've had it since I was nineteen, and I'd sell the coat and the watches and I'd sell most of what I believe before I sold that, and if you want to call that a character defect I won't argue, because it is one."

Cas sat back and looked at me for a while, and Cas looking at you isn't comfortable, because she's doing sums and doesn't pretend otherwise.

"Then you will take work you would not otherwise take," she said, "and you will do it for two years and it will make you worse, and I have watched three men do exactly that, and not one of them noticed while it was happening. I would like it minuted that I said so in January."

---

I went to the Ledger House for the file on a job and got something else.

Verity Ash keeps the reading room and it's the only warm room in that building. She had my account of the gold world out on the desk, all nine pages of it, in the fair copy.

"Sit down."

"I'm all right standing."

"Sit down, Emmet."

I sat down.

"I have read every account you have filed for nineteen years. There are one hundred and forty of them." She put one finger on the third page. "This is the best thing you have ever written and it has a hole in the middle of it."

"It's complete."

"It is not, and I would ask you not to do that, because it is beneath you and because I am about to do something difficult." Verity does not use contractions, ever. It makes everything she says sound as though it has been checked. "You describe a village that cannot dig and cannot keep a fire and builds towers out of pumice. You describe blocks moved with two fingers and a boy holding still for four minutes while an object drifts towards him. Every one of those requires the same underlying fact and you never state it. You do not imply. You are the least allusive writer in this archive."

"Verity."

"I know where it went," she said. "There is one person in this city who takes payment in facts and does not write anything down, and you have been seen going into his room twice this month, and I have known for eleven days what you have been buying and who it is for."

---

I didn't say anything. There's nothing to say to that.

"So," she said. "Here is what I am going to do, and I want you to listen to all of it before you speak.

"You have committed the one offence this institution does not forgive. Not theft. Lying to the archive. Men have been ruined for less, and I have written the memorandum that ruined two of them, and both of them deserved it and I would do it again."

"I know."

"I am not going to report you."

The stove ticked. She had her hands folded on the desk.

"Say why," she said. "I would like to hear whether you understand it."

"Because you want the record complete more than you want me punished, and if you report me you never get the sentence."

"No." She said it flatly. "That is what I told myself for four days and it is not true. The Order would have the sentence out of you in an afternoon and you know exactly how. It is not the record."

"Then what."

"You gave away the best fact of your career to buy nine years of guesswork about a woman who is almost certainly dead," Verity said, "and I have decided that I would rather have that in the world than have the record complete, and I do not know what to do with having decided it."

She turned the page over, squarely, and lined it up with the edge of the desk.

"I have believed one thing my entire adult life," she said. "It is not a small thing to find out at sixty-one that I believe it slightly less than I believe something else. You have cost me that. I would prefer you did not thank me."

"I wasn't going to."

"Good." She got up and went to the shelf. "Now. You came in here for a file. Which one."

---

They came for me on the Wednesday at the top of Cadge Street in the hour after the lamps go on.

Two of them, and it wasn't complicated and it wasn't personal. A man announces twenty thousand crowns in a public house with a notary present and inside a week half the city knows he's good for it, and there is a class of person in Sill for whom that sentence resolves to *carries money*.

They were wrong about that and there was no way for them to find out except the way they found out.

---

I'm going to put down what I am, because I've read accounts by men who fudge it and it always shows.

I'm not a fighter in the way that word gets used in bars. I've never fenced, I've never boxed, I don't own a weapon meant for a person, and I've never in my life gone looking for one of these.

But nineteen years of retrievals is nineteen years of moving human beings who do not want to be moved.

That's the job. That is literally the job. Men who've gone soft in the head on a world with one color in it and will fight you rather than come home. Men who've decided they're staying and will put a thumb in your eye about it. A woman who broke my nose on a stairwell in the Four Hundreds while I was carrying her, and she was right to and I still took her out.

You learn where a person's weight is before they move it. You learn to end a thing in a second and a half, because on the far side of a door with a clock running you have not got longer than that. And you learn to do it without breaking anything, because whatever you do to them you are going to be carrying them ten minutes later.

So no, I'm not a fighter. I'm a man who has done it several hundred times and is unusually good at it, and there's a difference, and the difference is that I've never once enjoyed it and I'd give it up tomorrow.

They found that out over about four seconds.

---

The first one came at me on a flight of steps, so I took his weight and let the steps do it, and he went down them and stayed at the bottom. There is no skill in that. It was the steps.

The second got a hand on my left arm.

On the arm. Fingers round the scar, and I felt him find it, and I felt him understand what he was holding, and he made a noise like a man who's put his hand in water he thought was cold.

Four hundred yards of salt fused to green glass. People alive in this city who felt the ground move.

I don't know what I did. I know that when I came back to myself he was against the wall and breathing and my knife was in my right hand and there was nothing on it, and I know I stood there for a while with my heart going.

Then I got his face into the light to see if I knew him. I didn't.

I left him there and walked home, and sat on the edge of the bed for two hours with my sleeve up, looking at the thing in my arm.

That's the second time in twenty years somebody has had a hand on it. I've never written down what happened the first time and I'm not starting now.

---

# CHAPTER FOUR

The standing list is posted on the west wall of the Ledger House behind glass, and it's the only thing in that building anybody can read without asking.

I've stood in front of that case since I was nineteen. There are men in this trade who go and look at it the way other men look at the racing page, and three or four who haven't looked at it in years because of what it did to them.

The known worlds are the top two thirds and they pay what you'd expect. Forty crowns for a specific unanswered question about a surveyed populated door. Eleven for a language note. Six for a corrected water source.

Six. A man crosses to another world, walks to a well, tastes the water, walks back, waits out his whole window, comes home, writes it up, files it, and is paid six crowns. There is a fee for filing.

Then the unwalked column: numbers with nothing beside them, and figures that climb every year.

---

I need to say something about that column, because people outside this trade look at those figures and cannot understand why anybody is poor.

Twelve hundred to walk through a door and write down what's there. It goes up every year nobody does it and it goes up faster every time a crew goes in and doesn't come out. There are gates on that list paying more than a man earns in a lifetime.

And it looks easy on paper. You've got a window. You go in, you look, you write, and the stone brings you home whether you want it or not. It's a day's work with a guaranteed exit.

Every summer a few of them work that out and go.

What they find is that the window is unknown, so you don't know if you've got nine hours or nine weeks. And that a door can open onto a cliff, or a river, or forty feet under, or into a room with people in it who have decided something about you before you've finished arriving. It can open into weather nobody in Sill has a word for. It can open in front of an animal the size of a tram.

I know of one that opened in the middle of a battle. Not near one. In it. The crew came home on schedule with all their limbs and nothing in their notebooks except that, and they were paid, and one of them never went out again.

That's the job those figures are for. It isn't a wage for a day's walk. It's what the Order has calculated it costs to get somebody to open a door with nothing at all behind it, and the calculation is honest, and it has to keep going up because the previous number wasn't enough.

Two of the men I hired for Ada died on that column.

---

There is a bench in front of that case. The Order put it there, at its own expense, facing the glass, and in nineteen years I have not been able to decide whether that is kindness or advertising.

I read the whole board twice, which takes about forty minutes, and did the sums on every line two people could do, and every one came out the same. Years, and only if nothing went wrong, and something always goes wrong.

Then I read it a third time and found the thing I'd been walking past for a fortnight, because it was in the wrong column.

It was in the known worlds. Surveyed. Populated. Walked more than a hundred times.

Eight thousand crowns.

---

Gate 1,046.

I went in and asked for the file and Verity gave it to me without a word, which was its own kind of remark.

It's hill country with terraced farming and about four hundred thousand people, and it's one of the great honest gates. For three hundred years, every Sill body that died on that ground came home. Wrapped in a red twill they weave themselves, stone bound at the throat under wax, and a hand-sign done at the arch by whoever carried them.

Two hundred and nine returns in three hundred years. The Order has a liturgy for receiving them that's older than the liturgy for anything else.

The reason for all of it is in one line near the top of the file and it's the only cheerful thing in the folder. The window is twenty to twenty-four days. It's the longest anybody has ever measured anywhere.

Which means you can die at the top of a terrace three days' walk from the arch and still be carried down, and washed, and wrapped, and signed over, before the people carrying you have to leave. Every other honest gate in that archive is honest because somebody over there decided to be. That one is honest because the arithmetic allowed it.

Six years ago they stopped.

Nothing has come back through 1,046 since. Not a body, not an object, not a letter.

And not a person.

That took me three passes to read properly, because the file puts it in the same voice it uses for rainfall. Eleven Sill crews have gone through in six years. The clerk logged all eleven of them in. The clerk has not logged one of them out.

I want to be plain about what that sentence means, because it isn't a sentence about eleven unlucky crews.

A stone brings you home whether you want it or not. That is the whole trade. It is the one thing in this business that has never had an exception anybody can name, and the men who go through unwalked doors for money are betting their lives on it every summer and winning. Eleven crews. Fifteen people, fifteen stones, and not one of them has put anybody out of that arch.

Nobody has gone through 1,046 in two years. There's a clerk sitting at a door that used to take a hundred crews a year, with a stove and a full kettle and a book with nothing written in it, and the heap of left-behind things at the foot of that gate hasn't grown since the spring before last.

The Order wants to know why.

---

Eight thousand crowns for a question about a mapped world does not happen. They pay nineteen hundred for a door nobody has ever opened.

So they're offering four times that for a walk into a place with roads and a market and a language everybody's grandmother could half speak, and that tells you exactly how frightened they are, and the file says none of it. The file is nine words on the subject and the nine words are entirely reasonable.

Here's what I understood standing in that reading room.

A real fraction of the Order's supply of stones arrives as the grief of strangers. Every returned body is a stranded community giving away the one object that could have sent one of their own children anywhere.

And fifteen stones have gone through that door in six years and stayed on the far side of it. In that building that is not fifteen people. It is fifteen objects nobody can make.

So a gate going quiet isn't an accident to them. It's a bereavement and an audit at the same time, and they've been sitting with it for six years, and that figure on the board is what fear looks like when it has to be written as a number.

---

Two people. That's what the job takes and it's why nobody's done it.

You can't send a crew. Six armed strangers walking into a market town on a world that's stopped speaking to us is a guaranteed way to learn nothing, and eleven versions of that idea are still over there.

What it needs is somebody who can walk in and be recognized. Somebody who knows the terraces and the market days and the way the language has rotted on that side. Somebody who can sit down in a kitchen and ask why the wrapping stopped and be answered.

There's one person in this city who has that, and she hasn't been through a door in thirty years.

---

Mother Ansel was stranded on 1,046 for nine years.

Everybody in the trade knows the shape of it and nobody knows the middle. She went out at twenty-six, her stone embered on the fourth day, and she stood in a field and watched four people vanish. Nine years later a survey party came through and one of them gave her his and stayed, which is the most respected act in this profession and everybody privately thinks the people who do it are lunatics.

She came home at thirty-five, took the lease on the Lazaret, and has stood behind those taps ever since. She wears a clear stone openly on a cord at her throat and hasn't spent a crossing on it in thirty years.

I've been out to get her twice since. Both times somebody else had put her there. She's never thanked me for either and never will and I'd rather she didn't.

---

I went in on a Thursday afternoon with the file, and she was drying a glass that was dry.

"1,046 has gone quiet," I said.

"Six years in March. Two months from now, if you want it exact."

I stopped with my hand on the file.

"You knew."

"I have known since the third month." She put the glass down. "A hundred crews a year came through that door and every one of them drank in this room. The cloth stopped and the drinkers stopped in the same season. You do not need to be clever to notice an empty stool. You need to be standing in one place, which is the only qualification I have ever had."

"You never said anything."

"To who?" Ansel said. "Sit down, you are looming."

---

I sat down. She came round the end of the bar, which she never does, and sat opposite me, which she has never done.

"Six years," she said, "and not one person from that institution has walked into this building and asked me a question. Not one. There are nine survivors of long strandings alive in this city and they know every one of our names because they keep a list, and they have a room full of men who write on cards for a living."

"They'd say the account you filed at thirty-five covers it."

"I filed forty pages at thirty-five." She said it without heat, which was worse. "Nine years on one hillside and they wanted the water and the crops and the language and the burial rite, and they got all of it, and it is the best thing in that archive on that world and I have never once been asked a follow-up question."

"I'm asking you now."

"You are asking me now because there is eight thousand crowns on it," Ansel said, "and I would like you to know that I would think less of you if you pretended otherwise."

---

"Four each," I said.

"No."

"Ansel."

"You are not listening, which is usual. I do not want the money." She put both hands flat on the table. "You keep it. All of it. Eight thousand, and I will sign whatever the Order needs to say so."

"That's not going to happen."

"It is exactly what is going to happen, and here is what I want instead, and it is three things and you will agree to all three."

"Go on."

"One. Passage out and passage back. My stone has been on a cord for thirty years and it has been assayed once, badly, by a dead man, and I am fifty-nine. I am not going through that door on it and I am not asking you to lend me one. I want your arm. When it goes, whichever of us goes first, I want to be holding on to you and I want you holding on to me, and I want it agreed now rather than discussed out there."

"Agreed."

"Two. There is a letter. There is a name written on the outside of it and you may read the name, because if I cannot deliver it then you will, and you will do that before you do one single thing about eight thousand crowns. You will not read what is inside and you will not ask me who they are."

"Whose name is it?"

"You will see it when I hand it to you," Ansel said, "and you will not say it out loud in this bar."

"Agreed."

"Three," she said. "I am in charge."

---

"Of the trip."

"Of everything. Every hour of it. If I say we do not go up the terrace road, we do not go up the terrace road and you do not ask a second time. If I tell you to sit in a kitchen and say nothing for four hours you will sit in a kitchen and say nothing for four hours, which will very nearly kill you and I intend to watch."

"You could just have taken the four thousand."

"I could have taken the four thousand," Ansel said, "and then I would have spent six weeks arguing with the Gray Man about doors. Instead I am going to spend six weeks telling the Gray Man to carry things, and then I am going to come back to this bar and tell every single person who comes in here about it for the rest of my life."

I laughed. I couldn't help it.

"That is worth considerably more than four thousand crowns to me," she said, "and you have no idea how long I have been waiting."

---

She got up and went back behind the taps and started on the same glass.

"Settle your tab first," she said.

"I haven't got a tab."

"You have a tab from 1,411 and you have never once looked at it, because you are the sort of man who buys other people's beer and forgets his own, and it has been on the slate behind that door for eleven years, and it is nine crowns and four."

"Nine crowns."

"Nine and four. And I want it before we go, in coin, because if we do not come back I am not having the next person who runs this bar find it and think I was soft."

---

I paid it. Nine crowns and four out of eight hundred and forty, against twenty thousand.

Then she pulled the cord out from under her collar with two fingers and had a look at it, which I have never seen her do, and about four hundred people in this city have made a joke about that stone within her hearing and none of them has made it twice.

She let it drop against her collarbone and didn't put it away.

"March," she said. "We go in March. They cut the low terraces in March and there will be four hundred people out on that hillside who know exactly what happened, and one of them will say it to me, because I will be an old woman with the right accent asking about the weather."

"Eleven crews have gone through that door in six years and not one of them has come out."

"I can count." She put the cloth down. "Be at Gate One on the fourth with your boots seen to and your affairs in order, and do not bring the girl."

Then she looked up. That's the second time in nineteen years.

"They stopped sending their dead home, Emmet," she said. "That is not a thing you stop. I lived there nine years. I have been trying for six of them to think of a reason and there is not one, and I would like you to understand that I am not doing this for you, or for your sister, or for a number on a wall."

"No."

"I am going back to find out what happened to my friends," she said, "and you are coming because you are useful."

---

I walked home along the front of the Salt with the light going, and the whole field out there on my left, ten thousand arches in a line running east until they were too small to count, and a hut beside every one with a light in it and somebody in it with a book.

Four doors out there, and two crews about to race each other down them for money I don't have.

And a hillside in March that has stopped sending its dead home.

I've got a stone in a tin that cost me four years of rent, and I'd bought it so I'd never have to work alone again, and I always assumed the person it went on would be somebody young that I was teaching.

It's going in a fifty-nine-year-old woman's pocket, and she's going to tell me what to do with it, and I have not looked forward to anything so much in twenty years.
